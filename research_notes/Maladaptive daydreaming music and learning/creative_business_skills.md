## 1. Creativity and ideation science: quantity, brainstorming, divergent vs. convergent thinking, idea selection

### Takeaway
More attempts produce more hits (equal-odds rule), ideas should be generated alone first and then pooled/selected (nominal/hybrid groups beat interactive brainstorming), and idea *selection* is a separate, weaker skill: creators are decent judges of peers' ideas but not of their own, so a creator needs outside judges and market tests rather than gut feel about their own concepts.

### Cited Findings
- **Equal-odds rule [PR]:** Simonton (1997, *Psychological Review*) defined the rule as "the relationship between the number of hits (i.e., creative successes) and the total number of works produced in a given time period is positive, linear, stochastic, and stable" — i.e., the hit *rate* does not rise with productivity, so more output yields more hits. Quoted in Jung et al. 2015 — [Jung et al., Frontiers in Psychology 2015 (PMC)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4479710/)
- **Behavioral test of quantity → quality [PR]:** In 246 participants (ages 16–31), the number of responses on divergent-thinking tasks correlated r = 0.73 with judged creativity (three independent judges) — [Jung et al. 2015](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4479710/)
- **Caveat [Secondary]:** Not all studies support the quantity-breeds-quality hypothesis; tests report "mixed results of either a positive or no relationship between the production of ideas and their quality" — [summary of literature via search result referencing Jung et al./Frontiers 2019](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.00355/full)
- **Simonton quote [Secondary, popular press]:** After studying 2,000+ eminent scientists, Simonton told the LA Times (1988) the most important shared quality was "the willingness to produce a tremendous amount of work — often on seemingly trivial or useless projects — without fear of failure." — [Mental Garden Substack (secondary)](https://followmentalgarden.substack.com/p/equal-odds-rule)
- **Brainstorming productivity loss [PR]:** Diehl & Stroebe (1987, *JPSP*) ran four experiments testing free riding, evaluation apprehension and production blocking as explanations for why real brainstorming groups produce fewer ideas than *nominal* groups (individuals working alone whose ideas are pooled); production blocking (only one person can speak at a time, so people forget or self-censor ideas while waiting) was identified as the main cause — [Diehl & Stroebe 1987 PDF](https://homepages.se.edu/cvonbergen/files/2013/01/Productivity-Loss-In-Brainstorming_Toward-the-Solution-of-a-Riddle.pdf)
- **Hybrid process beats team process [PR]:** Girotra, Terwiesch & Ulrich (2010, *Management Science* 56(4)) found that a "hybrid" structure (individuals first work independently, then work together) generated more ideas, better ideas, and *better discerned* the quality of ideas than teams working together throughout. "Building on others' ideas" was counterproductive: teams showing such buildup neither created more ideas nor better ones. Quality of the best idea depends on (1) average quality, (2) number of ideas, (3) variance, and (4) ability to discern quality — [Wharton Mack Institute](https://mackinstitute.wharton.upenn.edu/2010/idea-generation-and-the-quality-of-the-best-idea/)
- **Creative forecasting / idea selection [PR]:** Berg (2016, *Administrative Science Quarterly*): field study of 339 circus-arts professionals; forecasts of new acts' success checked against 13,248 audience members. Creators were more accurate than managers when forecasting **others'** novel ideas but **not their own**; the mechanism (shown in a lab experiment) is the divergent-thinking emphasis of the creator role. Practical implication: let creators vote on peers' ideas — [Wharton Mack Institute summary](https://mackinstitute.wharton.upenn.edu/2016/balancing-on-the-creative-high-wire/); [Stanford GSB](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/insights/managers-are-not-best-judge-creative-ideas)
- **AI-assisted ideation narrows the pool [PR]:** Doshi & Hauser (2024, *Science Advances*): writers given LLM story ideas produced stories rated more creative, better written and more enjoyable — almost all the gain went to the least creative writers — but AI-enabled stories were *more similar to each other* ("individually better off, but collectively a narrower scope of novel content") — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11244532)
- **Prompting can restore AI idea variance [WP]:** Meincke, Mollick & Terwiesch (2024, Wharton working paper): pools of GPT-4 ideas from plausible prompts are less diverse than ideas from groups of humans, but prompt engineering substantially improves diversity; chain-of-thought prompting gave the most unique ideas and came close to human groups — [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=4708466); [Wharton Mack Institute](https://mackinstitute.wharton.upenn.edu/2024/new-paper-on-prompting-diverse-ideas-increasing-ai-idea-variance/)

### Inferences
- Ideation is a numbers game *and* a filtering game. Because hit rate is roughly constant per attempt (equal-odds), the controllable lever is the number of genuine attempts (ideas, titles, thumbnails, videos) — but the best-idea outcome also depends on variance and discernment (Girotra et al.), so generation and selection must be separate phases.
- Berg's result is directly relevant to a creator who daydreams about success: one's own ideas feel better than they are. Selection should be outsourced to (a) peer creators judging each other's ideas, (b) audience data (Test & Compare, outlier comparisons), not one's own enthusiasm.
- AI raises the floor but homogenizes; if everyone in a niche uses the same model with the same prompts, packaging converges. Use AI for volume/variance only *after* generating one's own list first (hybrid structure), and push AI for variance (chain-of-thought, "give me 30 ideas that are unlike each other," different personas).
- **How to practice it:**
  1. Daily/weekly quota: e.g., 20–50 raw video ideas or 20 titles per video, generated **alone and fast**, no judging (nominal-group logic).
  2. Only afterward, a separate convergent session: score against explicit criteria (audience pain, curiosity gap, clickability, deliverability).
  3. Swap lists with 1–3 peer creators and have *them* rank your ideas (Berg); keep a record of predictions vs. actual performance to calibrate your own forecasting over time.
  4. Then run an AI pass for variance on top of your own list (not instead of it).

### Gaps
- I did not retrieve Simonton (2003) directly (the requested "Simonton 2003" — likely *Psychological Bulletin*, "Scientific creativity as constrained stochastic behavior"); claims here rest on Simonton (1997) as quoted by Jung et al. 2015.
- No study found that tests the equal-odds rule specifically on YouTube videos or titles; transfer is an inference.

## 2. Incubation, mind-wandering, and daydreaming: channeling imagination into constructive creative work (vs. escapism)

### Takeaway
Mind-wandering helps creativity under specific conditions: the problem must be loaded first (prepared), the break should be filled with an undemanding or moderately engaging task (walk, shower, chores), and ideas must be captured. Free-floating, idealized success fantasies do the opposite — they sap energy and reduce effort. The evidence-based line between "incubation" and "escapism" is: problem-loaded, bounded, task-linked, captured, and process/obstacle-focused vs. unbounded, outcome-only, idealized and never returned to work.

### Cited Findings
- **Singer's three daydreaming styles [PR review]:** McMillan, Kaufman & Singer (2013, *Frontiers in Psychology*): (1) *positive constructive daydreaming* — "playful, wishful imagery, and planful, creative thought"; (2) *guilty-dysphoric daydreaming* — "obsessive, anguished fantasies"; (3) *poor attentional control* — "inability to concentrate on either the ongoing thought or the external task." Positive constructive daydreaming supports future planning/rehearsal, "self-awareness, creative incubation, improvisation and evaluation," and meaning-making; meta-awareness ("the ability to take explicit note of one's thoughts") helps determine whether wandering thought is adaptive or disruptive — [McMillan, Kaufman & Singer 2013 (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3779797/)
- **Mind-wandering during undemanding tasks aids incubation [PR]:** Baird et al. (2012, *Psychological Science* 23(10):1117–1122): N = 145; after a baseline Unusual Uses Task, participants had a 12-minute incubation period (undemanding task, demanding 1-back task, rest, or no break). Only the undemanding-task group improved — and **only on problems they had already worked on** (repeated-exposure), not on new problems; the undemanding condition produced more mind-wandering but *not* more deliberate thinking about the task. Daydreaming frequency (IPI subscale) correlated modestly with originality (r = .22 repeated, r = .20 new problems) — [Baird et al. 2012 PDF](https://www.cmhp.ucsb.edu/sites/default/files/2018-12/Baird%20et%20al.%20%282012%29%20Inspired%20by%20distraction.pdf); [UCSB lab page](https://labs.psych.ucsb.edu/schooler/jonathan/node/260)
- **Incubation meta-analysis [PR]:** Sio & Ormerod (2009, *Psychological Bulletin* 135(1):94–120): 117 studies, mean d = 0.29 (low–medium). Divergent-thinking tasks benefit more than insight tasks; **longer preparation periods produce larger incubation effects**; low-demand interpolated tasks produce larger effects than high-demand tasks or no task. The authors caution that incubation as a practical technique "must be designed with care" — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19210055/)
- **Real-world creative ideas arrive during mind-wandering [PR]:** Gable, Hopper & Schooler (2019, *Psychological Science* 30(3)): 45 physicists and 53 professional writers logged ideas nightly for two weeks; ~20% of their most important ideas occurred while thinking about something else; these were rated as important and creative as on-task ideas and more often felt like "aha" moments — [UCSB lab](https://labs.psych.ucsb.edu/schooler/jonathan/node/1576); [APS](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/blog/the-meandering-path-to-that-aha-moment.html)
- **The "shower effect" — moderate engagement [PR]:** Irving et al. (2022, *Psychology of Aesthetics, Creativity, and the Arts*): mind-wandering led to more creative ideas only when participants watched an *engaging* (not boring) video; moderately engaging activities (showering, walking) leave room for wandering while constraining thought — [UVA news](https://as.virginia.edu/science-based-shower-routine-could-boost-creativity)
- **Idealized success fantasies sap energy [PR]:** Kappes & Oettingen (2011, *Journal of Experimental Social Psychology*), four experiments: induced positive fantasies produced *less* energy (physiological and behavioral measures) than fantasies questioning the desired future, negative or neutral fantasies — and a larger drop when the need was more pressing. "Positive fantasies allow people to embellish idealized paths to idealized future outcomes" rather than anticipating effort — [Kappes & Oettingen 2011 PDF](https://www.psy.uni-hamburg.de/arbeitsbereiche/paedagogische-psychologie-und-motivation/personen/oettingen-gabriele/dokumente/kappes-oettingen-2011.pdf); [BPS Research Digest](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/why-positive-fantasies-make-your-dreams-less-likely-come-true)
- **Process simulation beats outcome simulation [PR]:** Pham & Taylor (1999, *PSPB*): 77 UCLA students; those who mentally simulated the *process* of studying (5 minutes/day in the 5–7 days before a midterm) studied more and scored higher than students not using a strategy; subsequent research has found process simulation more beneficial than outcome simulation (imagining the good grade) — [Wissensdialoge summary PDF](https://wissensdialoge.de/wp-content/uploads/2015/12/wissensblitz_59_planung_und_mentalesimulation.pdf); [The Behavioral Scientist (secondary)](https://www.thebehavioralscientist.com/?p=9593)
- **Mental contrasting + implementation intentions (MCII / WOOP) [PR meta-analysis]:** Wang, Wang & Gai (2021, *Frontiers in Psychology*): 21 studies, 24 effect sizes, 15,907 participants; MCII improved goal attainment, g = 0.336 (small–medium); stronger when delivered interactively (g = 0.465) than via documents (g = 0.277); publication bias means true effect may be smaller — [PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8149892/)
- **Vivid immersive daydreaming ≠ creative originality [PR]:** West, Somer & Eigsti (2023, *Imagination, Cognition and Personality* 42(4)): in adults with and without autism, originality in divergent thinking was *negatively* associated with immersive daydreaming — [University of Haifa record](https://cris.haifa.ac.il/en/publications/immersive-and-maladaptive-daydreaming-and-divergent-thinking-in-a-2/)
- **Maladaptive daydreaming term [Secondary]:** Coined by Eli Somer in 2002 — [PsyPost](https://www.psypost.org/classic-literature-shows-maladaptive-daydreaming-is-not-a-new-psychological-trend/). (Popular-press claims that MD is "an indicator of creativity" in the same search results were not traceable to primary data and are not relied on here.)

### Inferences
- **Where's the line between incubation and escapism?** Combining the above, constructive incubation has five features, each tied to evidence:
  1. *Loaded problem first* — benefit appears only for problems already worked on (Baird) and grows with longer preparation (Sio & Ormerod). Daydreaming with no prior work on a specific problem is not incubation.
  2. *Bounded, low-to-moderate-demand activity* — walk, shower, chores, commute, a moderately engaging video (Baird; Irving). Not lying in bed fantasizing for hours.
  3. *Capture* — ~20% of pros' important ideas come off-task (Gable); without capture they evaporate.
  4. *Return to work* — the incubation is a pause in a work cycle, not a substitute for it.
  5. *Content: process/obstacle, not idealized outcome* — success fantasies (being famous, a viral channel) reduce energy (Kappes & Oettingen) whereas simulating the *process* (Pham & Taylor) and contrasting the wish with real obstacles plus if-then plans (MCII, g ≈ 0.34) aid action.
- For someone with maladaptive daydreaming, the useful reframe is not "suppress imagination" but "redirect its content and give it a container": imagine *scenes from the next video* (the cold open, a thumbnail composition, a viewer's reaction to a specific line, the obstacle you'll hit while editing) rather than *the life after success*. That converts outcome fantasy into process simulation and creative rehearsal.
- West et al. (2023) is a caution: vividness/immersion is not the same as originality; the creative value comes from externalizing and iterating, not from the richness of the inner world.
- **How to practice it (deliberately scheduled incubation):**
  1. Load: 25–45 min focused work on a specific problem (e.g., "hook for video X," "thumbnail concept for Y").
  2. Incubate: 10–20 min walk/shower/chores with the problem in mind lightly (Baird used 12 min); no phone feed.
  3. Capture: voice memo or pocket notebook immediately; one line per idea.
  4. Return: review captured ideas the same day and turn at least one into an artifact (a title, a sketch, a script line).
  5. When an outcome-fantasy starts, run WOOP: Wish → best Outcome → main inner Obstacle → if-then Plan ("If I start imagining the channel at 1M subs, then I write the next three title options instead").
  6. Track: count of captured ideas used in actual work per week (a concrete metric for "constructive" vs. "escapist" daydreaming).

### Gaps
- No controlled study found that tests incubation scheduling or MCII specifically in people diagnosed with maladaptive daydreaming; the redirection strategy above is inference from general-population research.
- Evidence on whether deliberately *scheduled* daydreaming (as opposed to scheduled undemanding breaks) helps creativity is thin; the studies manipulate break tasks, not daydream content.

## 3. Constraints, analogies/distant combination, idea capture, and whether creativity can be trained

### Takeaway
Creativity training works (meta-analytic effects d ≈ 0.53–0.68), especially training that targets cognitive problem-solving processes and uses realistic exercises; constraints help up to a point (inverted U). Idea journals are supported indirectly (ideas arrive off-task and must be captured).

### Cited Findings
- **Creativity training meta-analysis [PR]:** Scott, Leritz & Mumford (2004, *Creativity Research Journal*): 70 studies; overall effect Cohen's Δ = 0.68 (≈75% of trainees above the control mean). Programs focused on problem-solving (original solutions to novel problems) were most effective (Δ = 0.84); programs targeting attitudes/behavior least (Δ = 0.24). "Well-designed creativity training programs typically induce gains in performance with these effects generalizing across criteria, settings, and target populations." — as summarized in [Haase, Hanel & Gronau (accepted 2022) PDF](https://repository.essex.ac.uk/34344/1/CreativityEnhancementMethods_accepted.pdf) and [Edge Hill summary](https://www.edgehill.ac.uk/?p=198123)
- **Companion review [PR]:** Scott et al. (2004b), 156 studies, 11 training types: critical/creative-thinking training and creative-process training most effective; **training of analogies was least effective** — [Haase et al. PDF](https://repository.essex.ac.uk/34344/1/CreativityEnhancementMethods_accepted.pdf)
- **Newer meta-analysis for adults [PR]:** Haase, Hanel & Gronau (2023, *Psychology of Aesthetics, Creativity, and the Arts*): 332 effect sizes; average Hedges' g = 0.53 (95% CI 0.44–0.61); complex training courses, meditation and cultural exposure most effective (g = 0.66); cognitive-enhancing drugs ineffective (g = 0.10); enhancing **convergent** thinking was more effective than enhancing divergent thinking; large heterogeneity ("reversed effects occasionally") — [Haase et al. PDF](https://repository.essex.ac.uk/34344/1/CreativityEnhancementMethods_accepted.pdf)
- **Constraints — inverted U [PR integrative review]:** Acar, Tarakci & van Knippenberg (2019, *Journal of Management* 45(1):96–121), a cross-disciplinary integrative review (not a meta-analysis): constraints (rules, deadlines, scarce resources) affect creativity through motivational, cognitive and social mechanisms in an **inverted-U** — moderate constraint best — [City Research Online](https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/id/eprint/20459/)
- **Constraints meta-analysis [conference paper, unpublished]:** Damadzic, Winchester, Medeiros & Griffith presented "[Re]thinking outside the box: A meta-analytic review of constraints and creative performance" at APA 2022 — [search result reference](https://findscholars.unh.edu/display/publication545063). Not retrieved; findings not verified.
- **Idea capture:** see Gable et al. 2019 (≈20% of important ideas off-task) in section 2 — [UCSB lab](https://labs.psych.ucsb.edu/schooler/jonathan/node/1576)
- **Format borrowing across niches [Practitioner]:** Paddy Galloway: "Great formats don't need to be invented — they can be adapted" (e.g., Red Bull's drone vs F1 video borrowed from Carwow drag races; "$1 vs $100," "10 minutes vs 24 hours" transfer across niches); aim for "familiar, but unexpected" — [Colin and Samir](https://www.colinandsamir.com/resources/the-new-rules-of-youtube-from-paddy-galloway)

### Inferences
- "Can creativity be trained?" — yes, moderately, and the best-supported forms are realistic problem-solving practice, not personality/attitude work. For a YouTube creator, the realistic problem *is* the work: generating and selecting ideas/titles/hooks for real videos with feedback.
- Haase et al.'s finding that convergent-thinking training beats divergent-thinking training supports deliberately practicing *selection/evaluation* (section 1), not only brainstorming.
- Constraints: give each ideation session a moderate constraint (a format template, a single audience pain, a 6-word title limit, "must work as a thumbnail with ≤3 words"). Too open = blank page; too tight = no room.
- Analogical/distant combination in YouTube practice = cross-niche format borrowing (Galloway) — but note Scott et al. found analogy *training* weakest, so treat it as one tool, not the core.
- **How to practice it:** keep a single capture inbox (notes app/notebook) → weekly review that tags ideas by format and pain; run "constrained sprints" (e.g., 15 minutes, 20 titles, must use a comparison structure); monthly "format raid" of 2–3 unrelated niches to adapt one format.

### Gaps
- The user-cited "Acar, Tadik et al. 2019 meta-analysis on constraints" could not be located; the 2019 constraint paper found is Acar, **Tarakci** & van Knippenberg, an integrative review. The search tool indicated a meta-analysis showing a positive constraint–creativity relationship exists, but it was not identified or verified.
- No peer-reviewed study on idea journals/notebooks *per se* as an intervention was found.

## 4. Scriptwriting / writing skill: how writing expertise develops; imitation, modeling, and retention-focused scripting

### Takeaway
Writing expertise develops slowly through stages (knowledge-telling → knowledge-transforming → knowledge-crafting), requiring roughly a decade-plus of deliberate practice; the strongest instructional evidence favors explicit strategy instruction, specific product goals, peer assistance, and learning by observing models/readers — combined with spaced (not binge) practice and feedback. For YouTube scripts, the science of curiosity gaps and narrative transportation supports hooks/open loops and story structure, while specific retention "rules" are practitioner claims.

### Cited Findings
- **Three stages [PR]:** Kellogg (2008, *Journal of Writing Research* 1(1):1–26): novices progress from *knowledge-telling* (retrieve and write) to *knowledge-transforming* (writing changes what the author thinks), and professionals to *knowledge-crafting*, in which the author's ideas, the text, and "the prospective reader's interpretation of the text are routinely manipulated in working memory." Progress depends on reducing working-memory load through learning — [JoWR](https://jowr.org/index.php/jowr/article/view/759); [PDF](https://jowr.org/jowr/article/download/759/823/694)
- **Time scale [PR]:** Kellogg: "deliberate practice must continue for a minimum of a decade for an individual to acquire expert standing"; by 14–16 children have spent ~10 years on mechanics and knowledge-telling; "approximately a second decade of practice is needed to advance from knowledge-telling to knowledge-transforming"; for knowledge-crafting "several years are probably needed to acquire the domain-specific rhetorical skills"; most poets' earliest anthology work came at least 10 years after they began writing poetry (Wishbow, 1988) — [Kellogg 2008 PDF](https://jowr.org/jowr/article/download/759/823/694)
- **Deliberate practice components [PR]:** Kellogg lists: effortful exertion to improve, intrinsic motivation, tasks within reach of current ability, feedback giving knowledge of results, high repetition. Novelists quoted: Joyce Carol Oates ("I consciously trained myself by writing novel after novel and always throwing them out"); Norman Mailer (>half a million words before *The Naked and the Dead*) — [Kellogg 2008 PDF](https://jowr.org/jowr/article/download/759/823/694)
- **Spaced, not binge, practice [PR]:** "A common mistake of developing writers is to compose in marathon sessions or binges of massed practice"; this can produce writing apprehension and writer's block (Boice, 1985). "Professional writers learn to compose for just a few hours per day at most, but on a highly consistent daily schedule." Spaced practice lowers immediate performance but improves transfer (Schmidt & Bjork 1992) — [Kellogg 2008 PDF](https://jowr.org/jowr/article/download/759/823/694)
- **Practice in a professionally relevant domain [PR, via Kellogg]:** Accounting students writing as professionals for a professional audience with consistent, thorough feedback improved over two years; controls in other majors slightly declined (Johnstone, Ashbaugh & Warfield 2002) — [Kellogg 2008 PDF](https://jowr.org/jowr/article/download/759/823/694)
- **Learning by observing [PR, via Kellogg]:** Observation frees executive attention from execution; in a revision study, "much larger effect sizes were obtained when the writers learned by observing readers instead of doing revision themselves," best when observing readers respond to one's own text plus written feedback (Couzijn & Rijlaarsdam 1996). Schunk & Zimmerman's four-step regimen: observe a model → emulate → deliberate practice to automate → self-regulated adaptation — [Kellogg 2008 PDF](https://jowr.org/jowr/article/download/759/823/694)
- **Modeling and social feedback [PR]:** Zimmerman & Kitsantas (2002) studied 72 college students learning a sentence-combining revision skill, comparing observation of a *coping* model (gradually improving) vs. a *mastery* model (flawless), plus social feedback — [search-result abstract summary (page not opened)](https://www.deepdyve.com/lp/doc/DTwFU2RBbi). Kellogg summarizes this line of work: "observational learning from a model can produce large effect sizes. However, when feedback is provided then the effect of practice can combine with the effect of the model to yield truly impressive gains" — [Kellogg 2008 PDF](https://jowr.org/jowr/article/download/759/823/694). Zimmerman & Kitsantas (1999, *J. Educational Psychology* 91(2):241–250) proposed following *process* goals until a strategy is somewhat automatic, then shifting to *outcome* goals — [search-result listing (page not opened; verify)](https://www.scinapse.io/papers/2073834305)
- **Writing instruction meta-analysis [PR]:** Graham & Perin (2007, "Writing Next"; 123 studies, grades 4–12) found 11 effective elements in order of effect: writing strategies, summarization, collaborative writing, specific product goals, word processing, sentence combining, prewriting, inquiry, process approach, **study of models**, writing for content learning — [AdLit summary](https://www.adlit.org/topics/writing/summary-writing-next). Effect sizes reported by Kellogg: strategy instruction 0.82, peer assistance 0.75, mentor-assigned product goals 0.70, sentence combining 0.50, prewriting 0.32 — [Kellogg 2008 PDF](https://jowr.org/jowr/article/download/759/823/694)
- **Older meta-analysis [PR, via Kellogg]:** Hillocks found structured tasks with clear objectives, guided problem-solving and peer feedback were "four times as effective" as lecturing about writing — [Kellogg 2008 PDF](https://jowr.org/jowr/article/download/759/823/694)
- **Curiosity / information gap [PR]:** Loewenstein (1994, *Psychological Bulletin* 116(1):75–98): curiosity arises "when attention becomes focused on a gap in one's knowledge," producing a feeling of deprivation the person is motivated to resolve; curiosity requires some prior knowledge — [Stafforini abstract](https://stafforini.com/works/loewenstein-1994-psychology-curiosity-review/)
- **Curiosity enhances memory [PR]:** Kang et al. (2009, *Psychological Science*): curiosity about trivia questions activated reward-related caudate regions; people spent scarce tokens/waiting time to learn answers when more curious; higher curiosity predicted better recall of surprising answers 1–2 weeks later — [Berkeley neuroecon](https://neuroecon.berkeley.edu/papers/papers_files/Kang_PsychSci_2009.html)
- **Narrative transportation [PR]:** Green & Brock (2000, *JPSP*): transportation (absorption into a story through imagery, affect, attention) increased story-consistent beliefs and favorable evaluations of protagonists; highly transported readers found fewer "false notes" — [Gwern-hosted PDF](https://gwern.net/docs/www/pdfs.semanticscholar.org/39691cee32e52fbfc33f1a0316aa0d008cb699a4.pdf)
- **Transportation meta-analysis [PR]:** van Laer et al. (2014, *Journal of Consumer Research* 40(5):797–817): extended transportation-imagery model tested on 132 effect sizes from 76 articles — [City Research Online](https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/id/eprint/6755/)
- **Retention scripting [Practitioner]:** MrBeast production memo (leaked, ~2024): "we freak out so much about the first minute"; minutes 1–3 should hold "the most exciting and interesting content that is also very simple"; minute 3–6 is "the next most important part"; plan "re-engagements" (content that "fits the story and makes people genuinely impressed") to prevent drop-off; title and thumbnail "set the expectations for the viewer" so the script must deliver on them — [Alexander Jarvis transcript](https://www.alexanderjarvis.com/memo-how-to-succeed-in-mrbeast-production/)
- **YouTube's own "intro" metric [Official]:** YouTube Analytics' key moments report measures what percentage of the audience is still watching after the first 30 seconds ("Intro"), and flags dips, spikes and flat lines — [YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/9314415?hl=en)

### Inferences
- An adult creator is past knowledge-telling, but most YouTube scripting starts as knowledge-telling ("here's what I know about X"). The target skill is knowledge-crafting: holding the viewer's moment-by-moment interpretation in mind (where will they get bored? what gap is open?). Retention graphs are literally an external readout of the reader-model Kellogg describes.
- Imitation/copywork has support through "study of models" (positive but lower-ranked in Graham & Perin) and much stronger support through *observing readers react* and *strategy instruction*. So pure copywork (Franklin-style rewriting from memory) is useful but should be paired with explicit strategies (hook formulas, beat sheets) and audience-reaction feedback.
- Coping models > polished models (Zimmerman & Kitsantas' hypothesis; verify) suggests studying drafts and revisions of scripts, not only finished viral videos.
- Hooks and open loops have a solid theoretical basis (information gap; curiosity → reward, memory), but the specific "rules" (minute 1, minute 3, minute 6) are practitioner heuristics from one channel style.
- **How to practice it:**
  1. Daily, spaced writing block (60–120 min, same time), not weekend binges (Kellogg/Boice).
  2. Weekly "model study": transcribe the first 60–90 seconds of 2–3 outlier videos in the niche, label each beat (hook, stakes, open loop, payoff, re-engagement), then rewrite your own intro using the same beat structure (study of models + strategy instruction).
  3. Process goals first (e.g., "every intro has a stated curiosity gap and a payoff promise"), outcome goals later (e.g., "≥70% still watching at 30s").
  4. Reader-observation feedback: have 1–2 target viewers read/listen to a draft while you watch where they lose interest (Couzijn & Rijlaarsdam), plus peer review exchanges (peer assistance ES 0.75).
  5. Post-publish: compare script beats against the retention graph's dips/spikes; log what caused each dip.

### Gaps
- Zimmerman & Kitsantas (2002) outcome details (coping vs. mastery model results) were seen only as the abstract's hypothesis; the effect sizes were not retrieved.
- Graham & Perin's specific effect size for "study of models" was not retrieved.
- Search-engine summaries state that Graham & Perin found grammar instruction ineffective (while sentence combining helped); the AdLit summary I fetched did not cover grammar, so this is unverified.
- No peer-reviewed study found on YouTube-specific hook structures or the "minute 1/3/6" pattern; practitioner only.
- Benjamin Franklin's imitation method is historical anecdote; no direct experimental test of copywork for adults was found.

## 5. YouTube ideation and packaging as a learnable skill: analytics feedback loops, outliers, A/B testing, and making a "wicked" environment kinder

### Takeaway
YouTube is a semi-wicked learning environment (noisy, delayed, confounded feedback), so skill grows fastest when feedback is engineered: many reps, proxy metrics compared against your own baseline (typical retention, CTR ranges), outlier study, YouTube's built-in Test & Compare (which picks winners by watch time, not CTR), and peer review before publishing.

### Cited Findings
- **Kind vs. wicked environments [book/PR concept; secondary summaries]:** Hogarth (2001, *Educating Intuition*): in kind environments feedback is immediate and clear; in wicked ones it "may be missing, slow, distorted or biased," and people "gain confidence without gaining skill" — [Wikipedia: Robin Hogarth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robin_M._Hogarth); [David Epstein Substack (practitioner/popular)](https://davidepstein.substack.com/p/making-a-wicked-learning-environment)
- **What YouTube's recommender optimizes [Official]:** Signals include clicks, watch time (added in 2012 because clicks alone didn't mean viewers watched), "valued watchtime" from satisfaction surveys ("Only videos that you rate highly with four or five stars are counted"), shares, likes, dislikes — Cristos Goodrow, VP Engineering, Sept 15, 2021 — [YouTube Official Blog](https://blog.youtube/inside-youtube/on-youtubes-recommendation-system/)
- **CTR baseline [Official]:** "Half of all channels and videos on YouTube have an impressions CTR that can range between 2% and 10%"; new videos/channels or videos under 100 views vary more; high-impression surfaces (e.g., homepage) naturally lower CTR — [YouTube Help: Impressions & CTR FAQ](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/7628154?hl=en-GB)
- **Retention analytics [Official]:** Key moments: Intro (% still watching after first 30 s), flat lines, spikes (rewatch/share), dips (abandon/skip); "typical retention" compares against your 10 latest videos of similar length; data takes 1–2 days to process — [YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/9314415?hl=en)
- **Test & Compare (A/B) [Official]:** Test titles, thumbnails, or both, up to 3 variants; the winner is the variant "with the highest watch time" (not CTR), because "Great titles and thumbnails serve an important purpose beyond getting viewers to click"; tests take a few days to 2 weeks; outcomes "Winner," "Performed Same," or "Inconclusive" (if inconclusive, the first variant is kept); desktop Studio only; not available for Shorts, kids/mature content, private videos — [YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/13861714)
- **Metrics that matter [Practitioner]:** MrBeast memo: "The three metrics you guys need to care about is Click Thru Rate (CTR), Average View Duration (AVD), and Average View Percentage (AVP)"; know the title and thumbnail before producing; "Get rid of Netflix and Hulu and watch tons of Youtube"; "You. Can't. Get. Inspired. By. Things. You. Don't. Know. Exist." — [Alexander Jarvis transcript](https://www.alexanderjarvis.com/memo-how-to-succeed-in-mrbeast-production/)
- **Packaging leverage and outliers [Practitioner]:** Paddy Galloway (strategist for MrBeast, Sidemen): small creators spend "95% of their time on filming and editing" while top creators spend ~30% on ideation and packaging; "The difference between a million views and 28 million views is how you package it"; example of a thumbnail improved 30–40% yielding "40x more views per day"; "familiar, but unexpected"; widen appeal across Core, Casual, New audiences — [Colin and Samir](https://www.colinandsamir.com/resources/the-new-rules-of-youtube-from-paddy-galloway); outlier method (study videos at 5x–15x a channel's baseline, isolate what differed in topic, packaging, format, timing) — [OutlierKit (secondary, tool vendor)](https://outlierkit.com/resources/paddy-galloway-growth-strategy/)
- **Experimentation mindset [PR, from entrepreneurship]:** Training entrepreneurs to "articulate a theory, develop hypotheses, test them, and critically evaluate the results" improved revenue and pivoting decisions — [Bocconi news on Camuffo et al. 2020](https://www.unibocconi.it/en/news/entrepreneurs-who-think-scientists-get-better-results) (see section 9)

### Inferences
- **Why YouTube is semi-wicked:** per-video outcomes are confounded (topic, timing, packaging, algorithm surface, seasonality), feedback for long-form arrives over days–weeks, sample size per creator is small (one video/week), and survivorship bias distorts "what works" lessons. Kind elements: precise retention graphs, CTR per impression, built-in A/B tests.
- **Making it kinder:**
  1. *More reps at the cheap layer:* titles/thumbnails/hooks are cheap to produce in volume; videos are expensive. Practice packaging 10–50x more often than full production.
  2. *Faster proxies:* the 30-second intro retention, first-24–48h CTR vs. your own typical range, and AVP vs. typical retention band are faster, more local signals than total views.
  3. *Controlled comparisons:* Test & Compare isolates packaging while holding the video constant — the closest thing to an experiment a creator has. Because it optimizes watch time, it also guards against clickbait that hurts retention.
  4. *Baselines, not absolutes:* compare to your own typical retention/CTR (YouTube's own framing), and treat outliers relative to channel baseline (Galloway).
  5. *Pre-publish judgment from others:* peer creators rating packages before upload (Berg 2016) adds fast, if noisy, feedback.
  6. *Prediction logging:* write a predicted CTR/AVP band before publishing; compare afterward. This builds calibration and counters "confidence without skill" (Hogarth).
- **How to practice it:** weekly "outlier review" (pull 10 outliers from 3–5 adjacent channels, note topic/format/title structure/thumbnail elements); every video: ≥20 title options and ≥3 thumbnail concepts, run Test & Compare on 2–3 variants; maintain a spreadsheet of hypothesis → variant → result; monthly review of retention dips mapped to script beats.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed research was found on YouTube creator skill development or on how reliable Test & Compare results are at small channel sizes (YouTube does not publish statistical thresholds).
- Galloway's numbers (95%, 30%, 40x) are anecdotal practitioner claims reported in a podcast write-up; not independently verified.
- The MrBeast memo is a leaked internal document (widely reported, ~2024); authenticity is widely accepted but not officially confirmed in sources retrieved; its advice is optimized for very large-budget entertainment content, not educational/coaching channels.

## 6. AI prompting: how to get better at prompting and working with LLMs, and the risks (over-reliance, skill atrophy)

### Takeaway
Official guidance converges: be explicit, give context and the *why*, show 3–5 diverse examples, structure inputs, let the model reason, and — most importantly for learning — define success criteria and test prompts empirically. Field experiments show AI boosts speed/quality on tasks inside its capability "frontier," helps novices most in structured tasks, but can hurt on tasks outside the frontier, can hurt low performers who ask it for hard judgment calls, and — without guardrails — impairs learning. Using AI for explanation/inquiry rather than delegation preserves skill formation.

### Cited Findings
**Official guidance**
- **Anthropic [Official, retrieved Oct 2026]:** Before prompt engineering, have "a clear definition of the success criteria," "ways to empirically test against those criteria," and "a first draft prompt" — [Anthropic prompt engineering overview](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/overview). "Think of Claude as a brilliant but new employee who lacks context on your norms and workflows." "Golden rule: Show your prompt to a colleague with minimal context on the task... If they'd be confused, Claude will be too." Explain the motivation behind instructions ("Claude is smart enough to generalize from the explanation"). Examples are "one of the most reliable ways to steer Claude's output format, tone, and structure"; make them relevant, diverse, structured; "Include 3–5 examples for best results." Use XML tags to separate instructions/context/examples; set a role in the system prompt; "Prefer general instructions over prescriptive steps" for reasoning; ask the model to self-check against criteria; self-correction chain: "generate a draft → have Claude review it against criteria → have Claude refine" — [Anthropic prompting best practices](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/claude-prompting-best-practices)
- **OpenAI [Official]:** "Few-shot learning lets you steer a large language model toward a new task by including a handful of input/output examples"; supply reference context; "Reasoning models will provide better results on tasks with only high-level guidance. This differs from GPT models, which benefit from very precise instructions"; pin model snapshots and "add representative fixtures, tests, and evaluation checks before changing production prompts" — [OpenAI prompt engineering guide](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/prompt-engineering)

**Research on techniques**
- **Few-shot [PR, NeurIPS 2020]:** Brown et al., "Language Models are Few-Shot Learners": scaling improves few-shot task performance, sometimes competitive with fine-tuning — [NeurIPS](https://papers.neurips.cc/paper/2020/hash/1457c0d6bfcb4967418bfb8ac142f64a-Abstract.html)
- **Chain-of-thought [PR, NeurIPS 2022]:** Wei et al.: providing exemplars with intermediate reasoning steps improves arithmetic, commonsense and symbolic reasoning in sufficiently large models — [NeurIPS proceedings](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper/2022/hash/9d5609613524ecf4f15af0f7b31abca4-Abstract-Conference.html)
- **CoT for idea diversity [WP]:** Meincke, Mollick & Terwiesch (2024): CoT prompting gave the most diverse GPT-4 idea pools — [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=4708466)

**Productivity and who benefits**
- **Jagged frontier [WP, HBS 2023]:** Dell'Acqua, Mollick, Lakhani et al.: 758 BCG consultants (~7% of individual-contributor consultants). On 18 tasks inside the frontier, AI users completed 12.2% more tasks, 25.1% faster, with >40% higher quality; below-average performers improved 43%, above-average 17%. On a task chosen to be outside the frontier, AI users were 19 percentage points less likely to produce correct solutions — [MIT Sloan-hosted SSRN PDF](https://mitsloan.mit.edu/sites/default/files/2023-10/SSRN-id4573321.pdf); [Digital Data Design Institute at Harvard](https://d3.harvard.edu/?p=18394)
- **Writing productivity [PR, Science 2023]:** Noy & Zhang: 453 college-educated professionals, occupation-specific writing tasks; ChatGPT cut time by 40% and raised quality by 18%; inequality between workers decreased — [MIT Economics PDF](https://economics.mit.edu/sites/default/files/inline-files/Noy_Zhang_1_0.pdf); [Stanford SCALE](https://scale.stanford.edu/publications/experimental-evidence-productivity-effects-generative-artificial-intelligence)
- **Customer support [PR, QJE 2025]:** Brynjolfsson, Li & Raymond: 5,179 agents; +14% productivity on average, +34% for novice/low-skilled workers, minimal impact on experienced/high-skilled; AI disseminated best practices of top workers — [NBER w31161](https://nber.org/papers/w31161)
- **Entrepreneurs [WP; appears in Management Science]:** Otis, Clarke, Delecourt, Holtz & Koning: 640 Kenyan entrepreneurs given a GPT-4 WhatsApp business mentor; no average effect, but high performers gained just over 15% while low performers did ~8% worse; low performers asked the AI for help on harder business tasks — [Harvard Kennedy School CID](https://www.hks.harvard.edu/centers/cid/publications/uneven-impact-generative-ai-entrepreneurial-performance); [Berkeley Haas](https://newsroom.haas.berkeley.edu/research/gen-ai-experiment-shows-mixed-results-in-helping-small-businesses-grow/); supplementary file at [INFORMS/Management Science](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/suppl/10.1287/mnsc.2024.06909/suppl_file/mnsc.2024.06909.sm1.pdf)
- **Creativity [PR]:** Doshi & Hauser (2024, *Science Advances*): AI ideas lift weaker writers but homogenize output — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11244532)

**Risks: offloading and skill atrophy**
- **Learning harm without guardrails [PR, PNAS 2025]:** Bastani et al.: ~1,000 high-school math students; during practice GPT Base improved performance 48% and GPT Tutor 127%; when AI was removed, GPT Base users performed 17% *worse* than students who never had access; GPT Tutor (hint-giving safeguards) largely mitigated the harm — [IDEAS/RePEc (PNAS 122(26))](https://ideas.repec.org/a/nas/journl/v122y2025pe2422633122.html); [Wharton Knowledge](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/article/without-guardrails-generative-ai-can-harm-education)
- **Skill formation RCT [Company research + Preprint, Jan 2026]:** Anthropic: 52 mostly junior engineers learning an unfamiliar Python library (Trio); AI group averaged 50% on a follow-up quiz vs. 67% for hand-coders (~"two letter grades"); speed gain not statistically significant. Low-scoring patterns: full delegation, progressive reliance, AI-driven debugging. High-scoring: "generation-then-comprehension" (follow-up questions), "hybrid code-explanation," and "conceptual inquiry" (asking only conceptual questions, fixing errors independently) — [Anthropic research post](https://www.anthropic.com/research/AI-assistance-coding-skills) (arXiv 2601.20245)
- **"Your Brain on ChatGPT" [Preprint, not peer-reviewed]:** Kosmyna et al. (MIT Media Lab, arXiv 2506.08872, June 2025): 54 participants (18 in session 4), LLM vs. search vs. brain-only essay writing over 4 months with EEG; LLM users showed weaker neural connectivity, "struggled to accurately quote their own work," and reported the lowest essay ownership; LLM-to-brain switchers showed under-engagement. Authors frame "cognitive debt" — [MIT Media Lab](https://www.media.mit.edu/publications/your-brain-on-chatgpt/); [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.08872v1)
- **Critical thinking (correlational) [PR]:** Gerlich (2025, *Societies*): 666 participants, mixed methods; frequent AI tool use negatively correlated with critical-thinking scores, mediated by cognitive offloading; younger participants more dependent and lower scoring; higher education buffered — [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=5082524); [DOAJ](https://doaj.org/article/d5c17dd91ce64775a065095d8af730ce)
- **Knowledge workers [PR, CHI 2025]:** Lee et al. (Microsoft Research/CMU): 319 knowledge workers, 936 examples; higher confidence in GenAI associated with *less* critical thinking; higher self-confidence with *more*; critical thinking shifts toward verification, integration and "task stewardship" — [Microsoft Research](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/?p=1135061); [PDF](https://advait.org/files/lee_2025_ai_critical_thinking_survey.pdf)

### Inferences
- Prompting skill is mostly *specification and evaluation* skill: knowing what "good" looks like (success criteria), articulating context, and judging outputs. This means domain skill (scripting, packaging taste) is a prerequisite for good prompting — consistent with Otis et al. (high performers gain, low performers lose) and Dell'Acqua (failures outside the frontier).
- For a creator, AI is "inside the frontier" for: generating title variants, restructuring outlines, summarizing transcripts, first-draft descriptions. It is near/outside the frontier for: judging which idea will resonate with a specific Muslim-coach audience, original lived-experience stories, and taste calls — where over-trust is risky.
- The skill-atrophy evidence (Bastani; Anthropic; Kosmyna preprint) converges on one rule: when the goal is *learning*, attempt first, then use AI for explanation/critique; when the goal is *output*, delegate only what you could evaluate yourself.
- **How to practice it:**
  1. Build a prompt library (one doc per recurring task: ideation, title variants, script critique, transcript-to-outline), each with a success-criteria checklist and 3–5 gold examples from your best past work.
  2. Run mini-evals: same input, 2–3 prompt variants, judge outputs blind against criteria; keep the winner and note why (Anthropic/OpenAI eval guidance).
  3. Use the self-correction chain: draft → critique against criteria → revise.
  4. "Human-first" rule for core skills: write your own hook/titles first, *then* ask AI for alternatives and critique (preserves skill; adds variance per Meincke/Doshi).
  5. Weekly "no-AI rep" (e.g., one script section written fully unaided) to check the skill isn't atrophying.
  6. Ask AI *why* questions ("why does this hook work/fail?") — the high-learning interaction pattern in the Anthropic study.

### Gaps
- Dell'Acqua et al. was retrieved as a working paper; I did not confirm a later peer-reviewed publication.
- The Kosmyna et al. study remained a preprint in sources retrieved; small sample (18 in the key session).
- Gerlich (2025) is correlational/self-report; causality cannot be inferred.
- No RCT found specifically on learning prompt-engineering skill itself (e.g., whether prompt libraries or eval practice improve outcomes); recommendations rest on official guidance.

## 7. Creative video editing: building taste, rhythm and pacing; attention research on cuts

### Takeaway
Film-cognition research shows good continuity editing works by managing viewer attention (well-placed cuts go unnoticed) and that modern films' shot-length rhythms have converged toward patterns resembling human attention fluctuations. "Taste" is a perceptual-category skill best built by high-volume, *interleaved* exposure plus analysis, then closing the taste–skill gap through a large volume of edits (practitioner consensus).

### Cited Findings
- **Attentional Theory of Cinematic Continuity [PR]:** Smith (2012, *Projections* 6(1):1–27): continuity across cuts depends on visual attention; perceptual expectations can be matched across cuts without a coherent representation of space; explains match-action, matched exits/entrances, shot/reverse-shot, the 180° rule and POV editing — [UAL Research Online](https://ualresearchonline.arts.ac.uk/id/eprint/21187/)
- **Edit blindness [PR]:** Smith & Henderson (2008, *Journal of Eye Movement Research* 2(2)): viewers told to detect edits missed about a quarter of cuts joining two viewpoints of the same scene, rising to a third when the cut coincided with a sudden onset of motion; continuity-rule edits produced more edit blindness; most misses reflect inattentional blindness as viewers follow the narrative — [DOAJ](https://doaj.org/article/f74db40d8a69447fabf983ae63e788a3)
- **Shot-length rhythms and attention [PR]:** Cutting, DeLong & Nothelfer (2010, *Psychological Science*): 150 Hollywood films (1935–2005); shot lengths became increasingly correlated with neighboring shots and approached a 1/f ("pink noise") pattern resembling the structure of human attention; action films closest, followed by adventure, animation, comedy, drama — [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2010/02/100223121435.htm); [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0956797610361679)
- **Learning styles/categories via interleaving [PR]:** Kornell & Bjork (2008, *Psychological Science* 19:585–592): learners studying paintings by 12 artists learned artists' styles better when paintings were interleaved rather than blocked — even though learners believed blocking helped more; mixing highlights between-category differences — [Springer summary](https://www.springermedizin.de/article/10.3758/s13421-012-0272-7)
- **Rule of Six [Practitioner]:** Walter Murch (*In the Blink of an Eye*): an ideal cut satisfies emotion (51%), story (23%), rhythm (10%), eye-trace, 2D plane, 3D space; "Don't ever give up emotion before story..." — [No Film School](https://nofilmschool.com/2016/11/6-rules-good-cutting-according-oscar-winning-editor-walter-murch)
- **Taste gap [Quote, Practitioner]:** Ira Glass: "Your taste, the thing that got you into the game, is still killer," but early work disappoints; "It is only by going through a volume of work that you will close that gap and your work will be as good as your ambitions" — [Writers' HQ](https://writershq.co.uk/the-taste-gap-by-ira-glass/); [Jay Acunzo](https://jayacunzo.com/blog/best-quote-on-creativity-ira-glass-gap)
- **Exposure as inspiration [Practitioner]:** MrBeast memo: "watch tons of Youtube"; "You. Can't. Get. Inspired. By. Things. You. Don't. Know. Exist." — [Alexander Jarvis](https://www.alexanderjarvis.com/memo-how-to-succeed-in-mrbeast-production/)
- **Spikes and dips as edit feedback [Official]:** retention "spikes appear when more viewers are watching, rewatching, or sharing," "dips mean that viewers are abandoning or skipping" — [YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/9314415?hl=en)

### Inferences
- "Taste" in editing is partly perceptual category learning (what makes a cut feel invisible, a pace feel right). Kornell & Bjork suggests studying many examples *mixed together* (e.g., three editors' styles interleaved) and comparing, rather than binge-watching one creator.
- Recreating edits shot-for-shot is a form of "study of models" + emulation (Schunk & Zimmerman's observe → emulate → practice sequence from section 4); it isolates execution skill from creative decision-making, reducing load (Kellogg's working-memory argument).
- For talking-head/coaching content, attention-based cutting (cut on motion/gesture, cut when information is complete, pattern interrupts at retention dips) is the practical translation of Smith's theory; the 1/f finding implies pacing should vary in correlated waves, not uniform jump-cut cadence — an inference, not tested on YouTube.
- **How to practice it:**
  1. Weekly "deconstruction": pick a 60–90 s segment from an outlier video; log every cut (timecode, reason: emotion/story/rhythm/eye-trace), B-roll, SFX, text; then recreate it with your own footage.
  2. Interleaved reference study: compare 3 editors' styles side by side in one session.
  3. Volume: short daily edit reps (e.g., one 30-second intro per day) rather than only full videos (Glass; deliberate practice).
  4. Feedback: map each retention dip/spike to the edit decision at that timestamp; request 1:1 critique from a more experienced editor (cognitive apprenticeship).

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed study was found on how professional video editors acquire skill or on the effectiveness of shot-for-shot recreation as training.
- No research found linking editing pace specifically to YouTube retention; practitioner claims only.
- Murch's percentages are a heuristic from a practitioner, not measured data.

## 8. Brand building (including personal brands): mental/physical availability, distinctive assets, brand vs. activation, familiarity and similarity

### Takeaway
Marketing science (Ehrenberg-Bass) says brands grow mainly by reaching more category buyers and being easy to notice/recall (mental availability) and easy to buy (physical availability), using consistent distinctive assets; Binet & Field's IPA analysis suggests balancing long-term brand building with short-term activation (≈60:40 for consumer brands, ≈46:54 in B2B), though Sharp disputes the ratio's evidence base. For personal brands, the most relevant psychology is mere-exposure familiarity, similarity → liking, and influencer credibility/trust/parasocial relationships.

### Cited Findings
- **Mental and physical availability [Practitioner-academic, Ehrenberg-Bass]:** "Brands mainly grow through penetration, which requires mental and physical availability"; mental availability = buyers easily think of and quickly recognize the brand when buying; physical availability = the brand is easy to buy. Double jeopardy occurs because brand share depends mainly on mental and physical availability rather than differentiated appeal — [Ehrenberg-Bass Institute (marketingscience.info)](https://marketingscience.info/how-do-you-measure-how-brands-grow/); [Byron Sharp blog on Double Jeopardy](https://byronsharp.wordpress.com/2014/09/29/what-causes-the-double-jeopardy-law/)
- **Reach and always-on [Practitioner-academic]:** Sharp (Aug 2022): "reach everybody and be always on with advertising in order to grow"; maintain mental availability consistently over years to "catch buyers when they fall" into the market — [Ehrenberg-Bass news](https://marketingscience.info/news-and-insights/prof-byron-sharp-skewers-binet-tells-marketers-to-sack-agencies-preaching-share-of-voice)
- **Light buyers [Secondary]:** most buyers of successful brands are light/occasional buyers; growth comes from attracting many of them — [Think Insights (secondary)](https://thinkinsights.net/strategy/ehrenberg-bass-theory)
- **Distinctive assets [Secondary summary of Romaniuk]:** Jenni Romaniuk (Ehrenberg-Bass): distinctive assets need both *fame* (how many recognize it) and *uniqueness* (how exclusively it links to your brand); she developed the Distinctive Asset Grid; mental availability is built around category entry points (the situations in which buyers think of the category) — [Marketing Architects (secondary)](https://www.marketingarchitects.com/blog/v41-n3-your-brand-assets-may-be-working-against-you); [University of Adelaide profile](https://adelaide.edu.au/people/jenni.romaniuk)
- **Brand vs. activation [Industry research, IPA]:** Binet & Field, *The Long and the Short of It* (IPA 2013): 996 IPA Effectiveness case studies, ~700 brands, 83 sectors, 30+ years; optimum ≈60:40 brand-building:activation; 2018 follow-up found 62:38; emotional brand-building builds memory structures that raise baseline demand and lower price sensitivity, while activation converts them into immediate sales — [IPA blog](https://ipa.co.uk/knowledge/ipa-blog/the-next-chapter-for-the-long-and-the-short-of-it); [IPA publication page](https://ipa.co.uk/knowledge/publications-reports/the-long-and-the-short-of-it-balancing-short-and-long-term-marketing-strategies)
- **B2B context [Industry research]:** LinkedIn B2B Institute (2019) suggested ≈46:54 brand:activation for B2B — [The CMA](https://thecma.ca/topic/articles/2023/08/04/the-long-the-short-and-the-reality-of-b2b-marketing-part-1); [BLCK Alpaca (secondary)](https://blckalpaca.at/de/wissen/social-media/social-media-grundlagen-strategie/brand-vs-performance-marketing-95-5-regel)
- **Dissent [Practitioner-academic]:** Sharp called 60:40 "terrible, very misleading," arguing it came from "a very weird data set, which is award submissions" — [Ehrenberg-Bass news, Aug 2022](https://marketingscience.info/news-and-insights/prof-byron-sharp-skewers-binet-tells-marketers-to-sack-agencies-preaching-share-of-voice)
- **Mere exposure [PR]:** Bornstein (1989, *Psychological Bulletin*) meta-analysis of 208 experiments: exposure–liking relationship r ≈ 0.26; strongest for brief exposures to unfamiliar stimuli; liking can plateau/decline after many repetitions — [Wikipedia: Mere-exposure effect (secondary)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mere-exposure_effect)
- **Similarity → attraction [PR]:** Montoya & Horton (2013, *Journal of Social and Personal Relationships* 30(1):64–94): 240 lab studies; similarity effects moderated by proportion of similarity, centrality of attitudes and information salience, consistent with an information-processing explanation — [Murdoch University record](https://researchportal.murdoch.edu.au/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/A-meta-analytic-investigation-of-the-processes/991005635870007891)
- **Influencer credibility/parasocial meta-analysis [PR]:** Han & Balabanis (2023, *Psychology & Marketing*): 250 effect sizes from 53 studies; credibility, trustworthiness and perceived expertise most important for attitudes; credibility, parasocial relationship, homophily (similarity) and trust most important for behavioral intentions — [City Research Online PDF](https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/31583/1/Psychology%20and%20Marketing%20-%202023%20-%20Han%20-%20Meta%E2%80%90analysis%20of%20social%20media%20influencer%20impact%20Key%20antecedents%20and%20theoretical.pdf); [UEA ePrints](https://ueaeprints.uea.ac.uk/id/eprint/101563)

### Inferences
- Ehrenberg-Bass and IPA evidence comes mainly from mass consumer brands; applying it to a solo creator/agency serving Muslim coaches is an inference. The transferable principles: (1) be consistently recognizable (same visual assets, phrases, thumbnail style, colors, intro), (2) be present where your buyers already look (YouTube, Instagram, communities of Muslim coaches) consistently over long periods (mental availability), (3) make it easy to buy/contact (physical availability analog: clear CTA, Typeform/DM path), (4) reach beyond current fans (light buyers) rather than only deepening loyalty.
- For a personal brand, homophily/similarity and parasocial trust matter (Montoya & Horton; Han & Balabanis): shared identity (Muslim, coach-adjacent), shared values, and showing expertise via results (case studies, teardowns) are evidence-aligned levers.
- Mere exposure supports regular, repeated presence; Bornstein's "brief exposures" and satiation findings suggest variety within a consistent frame.
- Brand vs. activation: a B2B-like service (selling YouTube services to coaches) maps closer to the 46:54 B2B guidance — e.g., roughly half the content building familiarity/trust (educational, story, values), half direct activation (case studies with CTA, offers) — treat as a starting hypothesis to test, not a rule, given Sharp's critique.
- **How to practice it:**
  1. Define 3–5 distinctive assets (color, typography, catchphrase, visual motif, intro line) and audit every piece of content for them monthly; test recognition by asking a few audience members to identify your content with name/logo removed (a lightweight version of Romaniuk's fame/uniqueness check).
  2. Map category entry points: list the situations in which a Muslim coach thinks "I need help with YouTube" and make content for each.
  3. Case-study analysis: monthly teardown of one successful personal brand in an adjacent niche (assets, cadence, reach channels, offer path).
  4. Track simple brand metrics over time: inbound DMs mentioning "I've been watching you for a while," % of leads from content, unaided recall in conversations.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed research found that directly tests Ehrenberg-Bass laws or the 60:40 ratio for individual personal brands or solo creators.
- Bornstein (1989) specifics were obtained via a secondary source; the claim that liking peaks at 10–20 exposures was not verified from the primary and is not relied on.
- Romaniuk's distinctive-asset claims were sourced via secondary summaries, not her book.

## 9. Business skill learning: rapid experiments, entrepreneurship training evidence, mentorship

### Takeaway
The best causal evidence favors a "scientific" approach to business decisions — explicit theories, hypotheses, tests and critical evaluation — over intuition-driven trial and error; generic business training has modest, often statistically weak effects; AI mentorship helps those already competent and can hurt the weakest, implying human mentorship/diagnosis first.

### Cited Findings
- **Scientific approach RCT [PR]:** Camuffo, Cordova, Gambardella & Spina (2020, *Management Science*): RCT with Italian startups (116 per the published abstract summary; Bocconi's news release says 100), 16 data points over ~1 year; both arms got 10 training sessions on getting market feedback; the treatment arm learned to build theories, formulate hypotheses and run rigorous tests. Treated entrepreneurs earned more revenue, pivoted more, and did not drop out more early on; the approach reduced false positives/negatives in reading market signals — [SUFE academic newsletter abstract](https://academicnewsletter.sufe.edu.cn/info/391016); [Bocconi news](https://www.unibocconi.it/en/news/entrepreneurs-who-think-scientists-get-better-results); a large-scale replication/extension exists as a CEPR discussion paper — [DP15972 listing](https://causalclaims.trfetzer.com/paper/DP15972.html)
- **Business training review [PR]:** McKenzie & Woodruff (2014, *World Bank Research Observer* 29(1):48–82): owners implement some taught practices but improvements are "often relatively modest"; "few studies find significant impacts on profits or sales, although a couple of the studies with more statistical power have done so"; stronger evidence that training helps prospective owners start businesses faster; many evaluations have low power, short follow-up and measurement problems — [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/p/wbk/wbrwps/6202.html)
- **AI mentor heterogeneity [WP / Management Science]:** Otis et al.: high performers +15%, low performers −8% with a GPT-4 mentor; low performers asked about harder problems — [HKS CID](https://www.hks.harvard.edu/centers/cid/publications/uneven-impact-generative-ai-entrepreneurial-performance)
- **Practice in authentic contexts with feedback [PR, writing domain]:** practice framed as real professional tasks with thorough feedback produced gains (Johnstone et al. via Kellogg 2008) — [Kellogg 2008 PDF](https://jowr.org/jowr/article/download/759/823/694)
- **Cognitive apprenticeship / mentorship [PR, writing domain]:** guided participation, tasks in the zone of proximal development, and learning by observing a mentor — [Kellogg 2008 PDF](https://jowr.org/jowr/article/download/759/823/694)

### Inferences
- For a young agency/personal-brand business, the Camuffo protocol translates to: write down the theory (who buys, why, at what price, through which channel), derive falsifiable hypotheses ("≥3 of 20 coach DMs will book a call with offer X"), test cheaply, and decide in advance what result means pivot vs. persevere.
- McKenzie & Woodruff suggests not over-investing in generic courses; specific, practice-linked learning (with outcome tracking) is more defensible.
- Otis et al. implies the AI-mentor approach is safest when you already have enough skill to filter advice; for hard strategic calls, seek experienced human mentors (cognitive apprenticeship), and use AI for structured, well-specified subtasks.
- **How to practice it:** a monthly "experiment log" (hypothesis → test → metric → decision); 1–2 experiments live at a time (offer wording, price, outreach script, lead magnet); quarterly review with a mentor or peer founders; treat each client project as a deliberate-practice rep with a written post-mortem.

### Gaps
- Sample-size discrepancy for Camuffo et al. (116 vs. 100 startups) between sources; the published paper should be checked.
- Little rigorous evidence specifically on learning to run a creator/agency business; transfer from startup and developing-country microenterprise RCTs is an inference.
- No RCT found on mentorship per se for creators.

## 10. Cross-cutting: what expertise science says about practice itself (calibrating expectations)

### Takeaway
Deliberate practice matters but explains a minority of performance variance (and very little in professions), so practice systems should be combined with good feedback environments, mentorship, and choosing tasks where reps generate clear signal; spaced/interleaved practice beats massed/blocked practice for long-term learning even though it feels less effective.

### Cited Findings
- **Deliberate practice meta-analysis [PR]:** Macnamara, Hambrick & Oswald (2014, *Psychological Science*): 88 studies; deliberate practice explained 26% of performance variance in games, 21% music, 18% sports, 4% education, and <1% in professions — [Princeton news](https://www.princeton.edu/news/2014/07/03/becoming-expert-takes-more-practice); [APS](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/becoming-an-expert-takes-more-than-practice.html)
- **10-year rule and hours [PR, via Kellogg]:** expert typists/chess players/violinists need ≥10 years; best violinists accumulated >10,000 hours of solitary practice vs. ~7,500 / 5,000 / 1,500 for lesser groups (Ericsson, Krampe & Tesch-Römer 1993) — [Kellogg 2008 PDF](https://jowr.org/jowr/article/download/759/823/694)
- **Spacing and interleaving [PR]:** spaced practice lowers training performance but improves long-term learning and transfer (Schmidt & Bjork 1992, via Kellogg) — [Kellogg 2008 PDF](https://jowr.org/jowr/article/download/759/823/694); interleaving improved style learning while learners believed blocking was better (Kornell & Bjork 2008) — [Springer](https://www.springermedizin.de/article/10.3758/s13421-012-0272-7)
- **Wicked environments breed false confidence [concept]:** — [Epstein Substack (popular)](https://davidepstein.substack.com/p/making-a-wicked-learning-environment)

### Inferences
- Content creation and business resemble "professions" more than chess: practice hours alone won't predict success; the quality of feedback and the selection of what to practice matter more. Hence the emphasis across sections on cheap high-volume reps (titles, hooks, cuts), engineered feedback (A/B tests, retention graphs, peer review), and mentorship.
- Learners (especially those prone to fantasy) will feel that blocked, comfortable practice and consuming content are working; the evidence says spaced, interleaved, effortful practice with feedback works better even when it feels worse.
- **A weekly practice skeleton across all skills (inference):** daily 60–120 min spaced creation block (script/edit) → 2–3 scheduled incubation walks with capture → weekly outlier/model study (packaging + editing) → weekly peer exchange (rank each other's titles/hooks) → per-video prediction log + Test & Compare → monthly experiment review (business) and brand-asset audit.

### Gaps
- No study found that measures deliberate-practice effects for YouTube creators, editors, or prompt engineers specifically.
- The degree to which maladaptive daydreaming interacts with practice adherence (e.g., replacing practice time) was outside the sources retrieved here; another researcher's notes on MD should be consulted.
