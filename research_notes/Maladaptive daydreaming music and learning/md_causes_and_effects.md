# Maladaptive Daydreaming (MD): Cause → Effect Chains (Triggers, Functions, the Avoidance Loop, and Effects on Focus, Work and Productivity)

*Evidence labels used throughout: **[EXP]** = experiment, so a causal claim is possible; **[RCT]** = randomized treatment trial; **[LONG]** = prospective/longitudinal (multi-wave) design; **[DIARY]** = daily diary or experience sampling, which can show what follows what day to day but does not prove causation; **[XS]** = cross-sectional correlation; **[META]** = meta-analysis; **[QUAL]** = qualitative interviews, case reports or forum posts (anecdotal). Most MD evidence comes from self-selected online samples of self-identified MDers, about 75–85% of them female, so it may not generalize to a young male creator.*

---

## 1. Triggers: what sets off an MD episode?

### Takeaway
The best-supported triggers are (a) **evocative music**, (b) **repetitive movement** (pacing, rocking), (c) **solitude/idle time**, (d) **distress or boredom**, and (e) a **strong emotional pull** in either direction ("affective salience"). Daily-diary work found that obsessive-compulsive symptoms were the only consistent next-day predictor of MD. Day-level depression, anxiety and negative mood did *not* predict next-day MD, although they may act within minutes or hours, which daily designs cannot detect. Music is the trigger with the most detailed evidence. It starts the episode, deepens it and makes it harder to stop. I could not find a published percentage of MDers reporting music as a trigger.

### Cited Findings
**Formal criteria that encode the triggers**
- The proposed diagnostic criteria for "daydreaming disorder" (Somer, Soffer-Dudek, Ross & Halpern, 2017) list these features: "Daydreaming is triggered, maintained, or enhanced with exposure to music"; "…with exposure to stereotypical movement (e.g., pacing, rocking, hand movements)"; "**Often daydreams when feels distressed or bored**"; "Daydreaming length or intensity intensifies in the absence of others (e.g., daydreams more when alone)"; "Is annoyed when unable to daydream or when daydreaming is interrupted"; "**Would rather daydream than engage in daily chores, social, academic, or professional activities**"; "Has made repeated unsuccessful efforts to control, cut back, or stop daydreaming." The validation sample was 31 MD participants and 31 matched controls. **[XS/clinical interview]** — [Somer et al. 2017, Psychology of Consciousness (PDF)](https://delphicentre.com.au/wp-content/uploads/2022/01/Somer_et_-al_Maladaptive-_Daydreaming.pdf)
- In the same validation, every MDS-16 item discriminated MDers from controls with good-to-excellent AUC **except Item 16 (listening to music as a condition for MD)**. That item was weaker, though still significant. So music is common in MD but is not universal, and it is not the defining feature. The total MDS-16 reached AUC = .996. **[XS]** — [Somer et al. 2017](https://delphicentre.com.au/wp-content/uploads/2022/01/Somer_et_-al_Maladaptive-_Daydreaming.pdf)

**Movement**
- In Bigelsen & Schupak (2011; N = 90 self-identified excessive fantasizers, 75 F/15 M, aged 18–63), **kinesthetic activity accompanied the fantasies of 79% of participants**. **[QUAL/survey]** — [Bigelsen & Schupak 2011, Consciousness and Cognition (PubMed)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21959201/)
- A diary participant wrote: "Running, walking, and driving are more effective at invoking daydreams than sitting or lying still." **[QUAL]** — [Soffer-Dudek & Somer 2018 (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5962718/)

**Music (the specific mechanism behind "putting music on to amplify it")**
- Somer (2024) ran asynchronous email interviews with 41 probable MDers from 5 continents (11 male; mean age 31.3; mean MDS-16 = 61.4, well above the cutoff of 40). The theme "**Music enhances the MD experience**" covered most of the data and appeared in **36 of 41** participants. Two outlier themes were "Music necessary" and "Music not desired". The author concludes that music works by "distancing the person from the external reality, triggering complex visual storylines, deepening the altered state of consciousness, and intensifying the vividness of the daydreamed plot by invoking an emotional response." **[QUAL]** — [Somer 2024, Psychology of Music (PDF)](https://www.somer.co.il/wp-content/uploads/2024/08/somer-2024-calling-the-tune-in-maladaptive-daydreaming-the-impact-of-music-on-the-experience-of-compulsive-fantasizing.pdf)
- Under the subtheme "Music as an MD trigger compromises the sense of agency", participants said: "The music triggers the daydream. If there's music on, I'll be daydreaming"; "**I can keep myself from daydreaming so long as I maintain concentration, but in the presence of music, that becomes difficult**"; "It's harder to come out of daydreaming if I'm listening to music. **I can continue for hours.** Therefore, I usually avoid listening to music a lot unless I have lots of free time"; and "music will significantly increase my ability to daydream and **may cause me to daydream even when I am trying to maintain focus**." **[QUAL]** — [Somer 2024](https://www.somer.co.il/wp-content/uploads/2024/08/somer-2024-calling-the-tune-in-maladaptive-daydreaming-the-impact-of-music-on-the-experience-of-compulsive-fantasizing.pdf)
- Music also works as a deliberate mood tool ("Music is like a mood controller… helps me tame the angry bull that my mood is"). Somer describes a sequence: intense feelings → intense music → cathartic daydream → relief → switch to heartening music → positive daydream. An artwork from an MD community shows a woman at her desk "listening to music while attempting to focus on her work". The music-triggered fantasy produces "functional paralysis"; the piece is titled "When the Escape Became the Prison." **[QUAL]** — [Somer 2024](https://www.somer.co.il/wp-content/uploads/2024/08/somer-2024-calling-the-tune-in-maladaptive-daydreaming-the-impact-of-music-on-the-experience-of-compulsive-fantasizing.pdf)
- From the general (non-MD) literature cited in the same paper: heroic-sounding music produced "more positive, exciting, constructive, and motivating thoughts," while sad-sounding music evoked calmer or demotivating thoughts (Koelsch et al., 2019). Music also changes the vividness and content of directed imagination (Herff et al., 2021). **[EXP, cited secondarily]** — [Somer 2024](https://www.somer.co.il/wp-content/uploads/2024/08/somer-2024-calling-the-tune-in-maladaptive-daydreaming-the-impact-of-music-on-the-experience-of-compulsive-fantasizing.pdf)
- Somer recommends **stimulus control** for music cues, borrowed from addiction treatment. **[Expert recommendation]** — [Somer 2024](https://www.somer.co.il/wp-content/uploads/2024/08/somer-2024-calling-the-tune-in-maladaptive-daydreaming-the-impact-of-music-on-the-experience-of-compulsive-fantasizing.pdf)

**Solitude / idle time**
- In 21 phenomenological interviews, MD "could be activated if several requirements were met": **solitude was necessary**, and kinesthetic activity and/or evocative music "appeared to be essential features." **[QUAL]** — [Somer, Somer & Jopp 2016, "Parallel lives" (PubMed)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26943233/)

**Distress, boredom, overwhelm, and habit**
- A process analysis of MD (Burrell, Lawson & Christoff, 2025) names **affective salience**, "the tendency of one aspect of experience to stand out due to its association with emotional arousal", as "a particularly strong source of automatic constraint" that starts fantasy episodes, alongside **mental habits**. "Strong emotion can exert a pull on thought. The pleasure of emotional gratification also commands cognitive resources in an automatic way." A forum quote: "**my brain has sort of been trained to escape into daydreams whenever I'm stressed, bored, or overwhelmed**." The authors add that even when not sought compulsively at first, "over time and repeated experiences, the process of fantasizing to emotionally regulate can become habitual." **[Theory + QUAL]** — [Burrell et al. 2025, Behavioral Sciences (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12562237/)
- MDers "often report that real-world negative emotions prompt them to begin fantasizing as a form of escape or coping" (citing Somer 2002; Wen et al. 2024). **[QUAL, secondary]** — [Burrell et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12562237/)
- A sensory example: in Salomon-Small et al. (2021; N = 510), 49.8% of MDers endorsed being "bothered by certain sounds or noises." This is cited as a possible distress-driven sensory trigger. **[XS, secondary]** — [Burrell et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12562237/)

**What precedes MD day to day (diary evidence)**
- In a 14-day diary study (N = 77 self-diagnosed MDers from 26 countries; 81.8% female), **only obsessive-compulsive symptoms consistently preceded next-day increases in MD**: intensity B = 1.30, quantity B = 1.39, both p < .001. **Previous-day depression, anxiety, and positive or negative emotion did not significantly predict next-day MD.** The authors note that a daily design "may miss acute anxiety preceding MD." **[DIARY]** — [Soffer-Dudek & Somer 2018, Frontiers in Psychiatry (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5962718/)
- In a 13-month, two-wave study (N = 814), stress, anxiety and depression at T1 **did not** predict MD at T2. The authors suggest the time lag was too long, since "distress may have a more immediate effect on MD." **[LONG]** — [Musetti, Soffer-Dudek et al. 2023, Journal of Behavioral Addictions (PDF)](https://air.unipr.it/retrieve/ee690cdb-2925-44d9-844e-fe5ae6f644c1/2023_Musetti%20et%20al.%20JBAdd.pdf)
- Poor sleep did **not** predict next-day MD (B = −0.04, p = .452), although it did predict next-day ordinary mind-wandering (B = 0.20, p < .001). N = 126, up to 8 days, 869 daily observations. **[DIARY]** — [Marcusson-Clavertz et al. 2019, PLOS ONE (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6880993/)

**Stress context**
- During Italy's COVID-19 lockdown (N = 6,277 adults), **17.2%** screened as probable MDers. Probable MD was associated with higher anxiety (OR 1.59–2.60 across severity levels) and depression (OR 1.90–3.23). Interpretation: "Individuals are likely to take shelter in comforting daydreams in the context of stressful circumstances." **[XS]** — [Musetti et al. 2021, Frontiers in Psychology](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.631979/full)
- In Lucas & Bone's (2025) clinical formulation model, **precipitating** factors include life stressors, emotional states, "critical incidents", social anxiety and loneliness. **Perpetuating** factors include media cues ("especially music as 'emotional soundtrack'"), "hypnotic behaviors (pacing, rocking, character embodiment)," "difficulty sustaining real-world attention," attachment to fantasy worlds, and "pleasure and yearning to fantasize." **[Clinical model]** — [Lucas & Bone 2025, The Cognitive Behaviour Therapist](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/the-cognitive-behaviour-therapist/article/introducing-a-psychological-formulation-model-of-maladaptive-daydreaming/883AC5B2DFE48DD252A2FA2EB6B82EE9)

**Media (films, games, books, celebrities) as content sources**
- MD fantasies are often "centred on fictional characters that can be inspired by several media," and often include idealized selves and being admired by others (citing Bigelsen et al. 2016; Bigelsen & Schupak 2011). **[secondary]** — [Pezzi et al. 2024, Journal of Psychiatric Research (PDF)](https://art.torvergata.it/retrieve/0ffe267e-94d2-434c-b9e0-9f4f848cffc0/Pezzi%20et%20al.%2c%202024.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Chain for the user's work-session pattern:** work becomes hard, boring or uncertain → negative affect or boredom (the affective-salience pull) → a habitual urge to fantasize → **music is put on deliberately** → music distances him from the external task, adds an emotional soundtrack and deepens absorption → exit gets harder ("I can continue for hours") → the session is lost. Every link except the first rests on qualitative MD data plus the formal criteria ("distressed or bored"; "would rather daydream than… professional activities"). The link "negative mood → more off-task thought" is experimentally supported in the general population (Section 3).
- Music is better seen as an **amplifier and maintainer** than the root trigger. The Item-16 result suggests not every MDer needs it. For those who use it, though, accounts consistently say music lowers their ability to stop.
- Idle time and bedtime match the "solitude/absence of others" criterion. Bedtime is solitary, unstructured and often comes with music or phone use, so it is a near-ideal trigger setting.

### Gaps
- **No retrievable statistic for the proportion of MDers reporting music as a trigger.** Bigelsen et al. (2016) is paywalled, and none of the open-access sources I read quoted a music percentage. Only the 79% kinesthetic figure from Bigelsen & Schupak (2011) could be confirmed.
- No experience-sampling study I found measured *within-hour* triggers such as task difficulty or boredom just before an MD episode. "Task discomfort → MD" therefore rests on the diagnostic criteria, qualitative reports and general procrastination/mind-wandering research, not on direct MD experiments.
- The Wen, Soffer-Dudek & Somer (2024) longitudinal study of daily feelings and daydream valence ([DOI 10.1037/cns0000293](https://doi.org/10.1037/cns0000293)) could not be accessed beyond its title.

## 2. Functions: what does MD do for the person?

### Takeaway
MD reliably supplies **short-term rewards**: soothing and escape from distress, mood enhancement and emotional catharsis, relief from boredom, compensation for unmet needs (connection, recognition, competence, power), and rehearsal of an **idealized self**. It runs on both positive reinforcement (the fantasy is gratifying) and negative reinforcement (it removes pain). One cross-sectional study suggests it may dampen distress in the moment. Diary and longitudinal data show it is followed by *more* negative emotion and distress over the next day and the next year.

### Cited Findings
- MDers report "immensely gratifying fantasies involving such themes as romance, relationships with celebrities, **wish fulfillments, and idealized versions of self**" (citing Bigelsen et al. 2016). "MD also encompasses **negative reinforcements**, such as when emotionally distressed individuals soothe themselves with absorbing compensatory fantasizing." **[review of XS/QUAL]** — [Somer et al. 2017](https://delphicentre.com.au/wp-content/uploads/2022/01/Somer_et_-al_Maladaptive-_Daydreaming.pdf)
- MD fantasies are "often laced with emotionally **compensatory themes involving competency, social recognition, and support**." **[QUAL, N = 21]** — [Somer, Somer & Jopp 2016a](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26943233/)
- A grounded-theory study (N = 16) found seven themes: (1) an innate talent for vivid fantasy; (2) "daydreaming and social isolation—a two-way street"; (3) the role of trauma; (4) **the rewards of daydreaming**; (5) **the insatiable yearning for daydreaming**; (6) **shame and concealment**; (7) unsuccessful treatment attempts. **[QUAL]** — [Somer, Somer & Jopp 2016b, J Nerv Ment Dis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27002749/)
- In a multi-country sample of 539 probable MDers, childhood trauma was associated with using MD "to **distract from painful memories**." Neglect and emotional abuse were associated with daydreaming "aimed to **regulate emotional pain**." Abuse was associated with fantasies of "an idealized version of their original families." **[XS]** — [Somer, Abu-Rayya & Brenner 2021, J Trauma & Dissociation](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32845809/)
- In the same 539-person sample, people high in **grandiosity used fantasies for "wish-fulfillment of power and dominance,"** and those high in separation insecurity fantasized more about relationships. **[XS]** — [Somer et al., Personality traits and MD: fantasy functions (Haifa listing)](https://sdg.haifa.ac.il/?p=5722)
- Among 242 probable MDers, **BAS Reward Responsiveness** was "most prevalent," high **BIS** (behavioural inhibition, i.e., avoidance sensitivity) significantly predicted MD, and MD correlated positively with neuroticism and negatively with conscientiousness. The authors conclude that MD "is associated with traits linked to **avoidance**, emotion regulation and motivational tendencies towards **reward seeking**." **[XS]** — [Wall & Clotworthy 2025, Imagination, Cognition and Personality](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/02762366251352852)
- **Conflicting/nuancing evidence:** in N = 252 MDers (84.1% female), MD correlated **negatively** with depression, anxiety and negative problem-solving orientation *within the MD group*. MD statistically mediated a *reduction* in the effect of "difficulty pursuing goals when experiencing negative emotions" on anxiety. The authors read this as support for "MD as an emotion regulation strategy allowing the management and reduction of negative emotions." **[XS]** — [Current Psychology 2024, "MD as emotion regulation strategy"](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12144-024-06487-3)
- Clinical formulation of MD's functions: "Escape and soothing from discomfort; emotion regulation and processing; **wish-fulfillment and compensatory self-representation**; psychological mastery of unresolved trauma." **[Clinical model]** — [Lucas & Bone 2025](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/the-cognitive-behaviour-therapist/article/introducing-a-psychological-formulation-model-of-maladaptive-daydreaming/883AC5B2DFE48DD252A2FA2EB6B82EE9)
- MD "may create feelings of **agency in the inner world**—feelings that may not be accessible through engagement with the material and social conditions of everyday life." **[Theory]** — [Burrell et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12562237/)
- A summary of the 2025 meta-analysis: MD "can function as self-entertainment, distraction from boredom, and compensatory wish fulfillment" but "cannot be considered an effective emotion regulation strategy." **[META summary]** — [Search-result summary of Somer et al. meta-analysis, Kingston eprint](https://eprints.kingston.ac.uk/56752)
- Diary participants describe daydreams as "stories… for which I feel real emotion, usually happiness or sadness." **[QUAL]** — [BPS Research Digest on Soffer-Dudek & Somer 2018](https://www.bps.org.uk/research-digest/people-maladaptive-daydreaming-spend-average-four-hours-day-lost-their-imagination)

### Inferences
- For a creator having **success fantasies**, the most relevant functions are *compensatory self-representation* (an idealized competent, recognized, successful self) and *mood repair*. A business fantasy gives him today the feelings of competence and recognition that the real work only delivers slowly and uncertainly.
- The cross-sectional "MD reduces distress" finding and the diary "MD is followed by distress" finding fit together. MD helps **in the moment** (which reinforces it) and backfires **by the next day and over months** (Section 4).

### Gaps
- No experiment measures mood immediately before and after an MD episode in MDers. The size of short-term relief is unknown.
- I found no study of how *success/achievement-themed* MD content differs in outcomes from romance- or trauma-themed content (Oettingen's work in Section 5 is the closest analogue).

## 3. The reinforcement/avoidance loop and procrastination

### Takeaway
The loop is supported in pieces. (1) General procrastination research shows that **aversive tasks** strongly predict delay, and that people under distress give **short-term mood repair priority** over long-term goals. This is experimental evidence. (2) Negative mood **causally increases** off-task thought and attentional lapses (experimental). (3) In MD specifically, distress or boredom triggers fantasy, fantasy brings immediate relief or gratification, and the behavior shows addiction-like craving, failed attempts to stop and annoyance when interrupted. (4) MD is followed by *more* negative emotion the next day and more distress a year later, and the widening gap between fantasy and reality is "often eased with further daydreaming." No single MD study has tested the whole loop.

### Cited Findings
**Procrastination as escape from aversive tasks (general population)**
- Steel's meta-analysis (691 correlations): "Strong and consistent predictors of procrastination were **task aversiveness**, task delay, self-efficacy, and impulsiveness, as well as conscientiousness and its facets of self-control, **distractibility**, organization, and achievement motivation." **[META]** — [Steel 2007, Psychological Bulletin (PubMed)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17201571/)
- A secondary summary gives the task aversiveness–procrastination correlation as about **r = .40** across about 10 studies (not verified in the primary text). **[META, secondary]** — [Steel 2007 (U. Calgary repository)](https://prism.ucalgary.ca/handle/1880/47914)
- Sirois & Pychyl argue that procrastination "has a great deal to do with **short-term mood repair** and emotion regulation" and "often occurs when people are faced with tasks that are seen as aversive." They add that "the consequences of procrastination are typically borne by the **future self**." **[Review]** — [Sirois & Pychyl 2013, Social and Personality Psychology Compass](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/91793/)
- **Causal test:** in three experiments, "believing that one's bad mood was frozen (unchangeable) **eliminated** the tendency to… engage in frivolous procrastination (Experiment 3)." Conclusion: "when people are upset, they indulge immediate impulses to make themselves feel better… giving short-term affect regulation priority over other self-regulatory goals." **[EXP]** — [Tice, Bratslavsky & Baumeister 2001, JPSP (PubMed)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11195891/)

**Negative mood → mind leaves the task (general population)**
- Induced negative mood (vs. positive) "led participants to make **more lapses**, report a greater frequency of **task irrelevant thoughts**, and become **less inclined to reengage** attentional resources following a lapse." Positive mood "was associated with a better ability to adjust performance after a lapse." **[EXP]** — [Smallwood, Fitzgerald, Miles & Phillips 2009, Emotion (PubMed)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19348539/)

**MD-specific evidence for each link**
- *Urge/craving:* in Bigelsen & Schupak (2011), 25% of respondents spontaneously described MD as "a compulsive addiction that engenders irritation, anxiety, and even illness when curbed." Quotes: "I can't turn it off… maybe like a drinker who promised himself 'this is the last bottle'"; "Feels like an addiction. I am in control of what drug I want, but not how much I consume it." A confirmatory factor model found **craving/yearning** to be a distinct MDS factor. **[QUAL/XS]** — [Somer et al. 2017 (reviewing Bigelsen & Schupak 2011; Somer et al. 2016)](https://delphicentre.com.au/wp-content/uploads/2022/01/Somer_et_-al_Maladaptive-_Daydreaming.pdf)
- *Interrupted episodes pull back:* MDers "reported significantly stronger urges to return to fantasizing after being interrupted, compared to control participants" (Bigelsen et al. 2016). **[XS, secondary]** — [Burrell et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12562237/)
- *Escape origin:* in a video-recorded clinical case, "MD was developed as a strategy to cope with distress but led to uncontrollable absorption in fantasy, social withdrawal, and neglecting aspects of everyday life." **[QUAL, case]** — [Pietkiewicz et al. 2018, J Behav Addict (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6426361/)
- *Habit formation:* "the process of fantasizing to emotionally regulate can become habitual." After negative events, "the next occurrence of a negative event may become more likely to prompt the desire or pull towards fantasizing." **[Theory + QUAL]** — [Burrell et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12562237/)
- *Relief → later distress (diary):* a rise in MD was followed the **next day** by more negative emotion (B = 0.37, p = .004), more obsessive-compulsive symptoms (B = 1.04, p < .001) and more dissociation (B = 0.78, p = .001). Authors: the data "show that an elevation in MD was followed by an increase in negative emotion, strengthening the notion that in the long term (i.e., on the next day), **MD does not promote pleasurable sensations but rather results in psychological distress**." **[DIARY]** — [Soffer-Dudek & Somer 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5962718/)
- *Relief → later distress (13 months):* MD at T1 predicted higher stress, anxiety and depression at T2 after controlling for baseline distress. Distress did not predict later MD. The authors: "MD may be used as a means to escape from psychological distress in the short term that results in long-term impairment." **[LONG]** — [Musetti et al. 2023](https://air.unipr.it/retrieve/ee690cdb-2925-44d9-844e-fe5ae6f644c1/2023_Musetti%20et%20al.%20JBAdd.pdf)
- *Gap between fantasy and reality → more MD:* "Because of its compulsive, addictive nature, MD creates further distress associated with the **time wasted** and the **widening gap between the idealized fantasy and reality**, a disillusionment that is often **eased with further daydreaming** (Somer, 2018)." **[Expert review]** — [Theodor-Katz et al. 2022 (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9790222/)
- *Agency loop:* "the compulsive urge to fantasize can also undermine one's agency in everyday life, affecting work and relationships. This may perpetuate a cycle whereby compulsive fantasizers cede agency in everyday life, while reinforcing the automaticity that makes their engagement more habitual." Fantasy replacing real-world pursuits "may, in turn, increase feelings of loneliness and helplessness, generating negative affect, which can trigger more powerful and frequent urges to fantasize." **[Theory]** — [Burrell et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12562237/)
- *Escapist internet use as part of the same loop:* in a case of a 25-year-old male MDer, "excessive time spent in escapist activities led to functional impairment, thus in turn leading to increased MD and excessive internet use to relieve distress" (Somer 2018). **[QUAL, secondary]** — [Pezzi et al. 2024](https://art.torvergata.it/retrieve/0ffe267e-94d2-434c-b9e0-9f4f848cffc0/Pezzi%20et%20al.%2c%202024.pdf)
- *MD and procrastination directly:* in 201 medical students, MD correlated with academic procrastination at **r = 0.181** (p < .05), and with depression r = 0.245, anxiety r = 0.222 and stress r = 0.216. MDers showed more procrastination and distress, **but GPA was not affected**. The authors suggest "excessive daydreaming may serve as an emotional escape." This is a symposium abstract, not peer-reviewed in full. **[XS]** — [Karaağaç & Korkmaz 2025, Turkish Psychiatry symposium abstract](https://www.turkpsikiyatri.com/Yayin/27th-National-Clinical-Education-Symposium-Presentation-Abstracts/maladaptive-daydreaming-and-academic-procrastination-the-relationship-with-psychological-symptoms-in-medical-students)
- *Breaking the loop works:* the first RCT (557 randomized, 353 completers) tested 8 sessions of web-based mindfulness plus **self-monitoring**. It produced large improvements in MD (η² = 0.24), daydreaming frequency (η² = 0.22) and life functioning (η² = 0.15). Wait-list controls did not improve. With self-monitoring, 24% showed clinically significant improvement and 39% reliable improvement, and gains held at 6 months. **[RCT]** — [Herscu, Somer, Federman & Soffer-Dudek 2023, J Consult Clin Psychol (PubMed)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36634023/)
- The diary authors propose "**response prevention** techniques [ERP-inspired] may be useful for curbing or intercepting unwanted daydreaming." **[Recommendation]** — [Soffer-Dudek & Somer 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5962718/)

### Inferences
**Mapped loop (each arrow tagged with its strongest evidence):**
1. Uncomfortable work (hard, boring, uncertain) → aversive feeling. *Task aversiveness is a top procrastination predictor [META].*
2. Aversive feeling → urge to escape into fantasy. *MD criterion "daydreams when distressed or bored" [clinical XS]; forum and interview accounts [QUAL]; negative mood causally increases task-irrelevant thought in the general population [EXP].*
3. Urge → music turned on → deep immersion. *[QUAL] Somer 2024.*
4. Immersion → immediate relief, pleasure and a felt sense of competence. *MD as reward and negative reinforcement [QUAL/XS]; within MDers, MD is linked to less momentary distress [XS].*
5. Relief → reinforcement → habit (the urge comes faster next time). *Habit/automaticity theory [Theory]; craving factor [XS]; stronger urge to return after interruption [XS].*
6. Lost hours → work not done. *4.5 h/day; 57% of waking hours (Section 4) [XS/DIARY].*
7. Lost time → guilt, shame, "time wasted", a gap between fantasy and reality. *[QUAL/Expert review].*
8. → Next-day negative emotion, and higher distress at 13 months. *[DIARY], [LONG].*
9. → Lower mood → stronger pull to escape. *Supported for "negative affect → urge" in theory and qualitative data. Day-level diary data did **not** show negative mood predicting next-day MD, so this link is likely fast (minutes to hours) or unproven.*
- Tice et al. (2001) suggest a practical lever: procrastination is driven by the *belief* that indulging will repair mood. That belief can be contested by noting that the diary data show MD is followed by *worse* mood the next day.

### Gaps
- No MD-specific experiment manipulates task aversiveness and measures MD onset.
- The "lower mood → more MD" link is not supported at daily resolution (Soffer-Dudek & Somer 2018; Musetti 2023). Within-day experience sampling is needed.
- The Steel (2007) r = .40 figure comes from a secondary summary; the primary table was not checked.

## 4. Consequences: what MD causes or correlates with (focus, work, time, sleep, mood, shame, relationships, identity)

### Takeaway
The strongest **directional** evidence is that MD is followed by more **negative emotion, dissociation and OC symptoms the next day** (diary) and by more **stress, anxiety and depression 13 months later** (longitudinal). Distress did not predict later MD in either design. Time cost is large: MDers report about **4.5 h/day** or **57% of waking hours** (vs. 16% for controls). Effects on **attention and work** are well documented in self-report and cross-sectionally: MDers attribute their inattention to the pull of fantasy, high unemployment appears in clinical samples, and general mind-wandering research shows off-task thought hurts performance, especially on complex tasks. **Sleep** disruption is reported in qualitative studies but was **not** found at the daily level. Shame, secrecy, loneliness and low self-esteem are consistent correlates.

### Cited Findings
**Time lost**
- Bigelsen et al. (2016; N = 447: 340 MDers and 107 controls, aged 13–78, 45 countries): MD differed from normal daydreaming in "quantity, content, experience, controllability, distress, and interference with life functioning." **[XS]** — [Bigelsen, Lehrfeld, Jopp & Somer 2016, Consciousness and Cognition (PubMed)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27082138/)
- In the same study, MDers spent "**57% of their waking hours in daydreams on average, compared with 16%** for individuals without MD." **[XS, secondary citation]** — [Lucas & Bone 2025](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/the-cognitive-behaviour-therapist/article/introducing-a-psychological-formulation-model-of-maladaptive-daydreaming/883AC5B2DFE48DD252A2FA2EB6B82EE9)
- In the 14-day diary, participants reported **4.5 h** of active daydreaming on the first assessment day, "taking up over a quarter of their waking time." **[DIARY]** — [Soffer-Dudek & Somer 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5962718/)
- A 2025 position paper sets the proposed criterion at **≥1 h/day** and puts typical episodes at **30 min–5 h**. **[Expert consensus]** — [Soffer-Dudek et al. 2025, BJPsych position paper (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12038384/)

**Attention, focus and task performance**
- MDers "endorsed significantly higher rates of **attention deficit**, obsessive compulsive and dissociation symptoms than controls." **[XS]** — [Bigelsen et al. 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27082138/)
- "MDers have reported a **diminished ability to sustain attention externally** due to their tendency to give in to the lure of their inner fantasy world." MDers who met ADHD criteria "spontaneously reported… that their behavioral addiction to fantastical daydreaming **impaired their attention**. They viewed the attentional problem as a **resulting or secondary factor**." **[QUAL/clinical interview]** — [Theodor-Katz et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9790222/)
- A diary participant: "I often feel as if I just cannot turn off my mind, whether because I need to **concentrate in class, go to sleep**, or just find some peace in the world outside my head." **[QUAL]** — [Soffer-Dudek & Somer 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5962718/)
- An epidemiological interview quote: "It hurts my ability to focus." **[QUAL]** — [Soffer-Dudek & Theodor-Katz 2022 (Frontiers)](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyt.2022.871041/full)
- MD and ordinary mind-wandering correlate strongly: within-person r = .38 and between-person r = .66. **[DIARY]** — [Marcusson-Clavertz et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6880993/)
- General mind-wandering meta-analysis (k = 88 samples): "increases in mind-wandering were generally associated with **decreases in task performance**." The negative relation "was **more pronounced for more complex tasks**." People with fewer cognitive resources mind-wander more, and longer time on task strengthens that link. **[META]** — [Randall, Oswald & Beier 2014, Psychological Bulletin (PubMed)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25089941/)
- In the general population (2,250 adults, about 250,000 samples), minds wandered **46.9%** of waking hours. Time-lag analyses "suggested that… mind-wandering was generally the **cause, not the consequence**, of their unhappiness." Mind-wandering status explained about 10.8% of momentary happiness vs. 4.6% for the activity itself. **[Experience sampling]** — [Harvard Gazette on Killingsworth & Gilbert 2010](https://news.harvard.edu/gazette/story/2010/11/wandering-mind-not-a-happy-mind/); [Science abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21071660/)
- One distinction: during MD "the mind is not wandering off-task but instead **focused on creating fanciful scenarios** that can become time-consuming. These daydreams can **interfere with concentrating, meeting real-life responsibilities, and maintaining relationships**." **[Review]** — [Somer 2024](https://www.somer.co.il/wp-content/uploads/2024/08/somer-2024-calling-the-tune-in-maladaptive-daydreaming-the-impact-of-music-on-the-experience-of-compulsive-fantasizing.pdf)

**Work, academic and occupational functioning**
- MDers reported that excessive daydreaming "interfered with their **sleep** and compromised their relationships, **work, and academic performance**" (Bigelsen & Schupak 2011). **[QUAL survey]** — [Somer et al. 2017](https://delphicentre.com.au/wp-content/uploads/2022/01/Somer_et_-al_Maladaptive-_Daydreaming.pdf)
- Distress in Bigelsen & Schupak (2011) came from three sources: difficulty controlling the urge; "concern that the quantity of fantasizing **interfered with actual relationships and endeavors**"; and "intense shame and exhaustive efforts to keep this behavior hidden." **[QUAL]** — [Bigelsen & Schupak 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21959201/)
- In Somer's music interview sample (N = 41; mostly well-educated), **46% were unemployed** and fewer than 15% were married or in a relationship, "potentially demonstrating the functional and social challenges." **[Descriptive, small sample]** — [Somer 2024](https://www.somer.co.il/wp-content/uploads/2024/08/somer-2024-calling-the-tune-in-maladaptive-daydreaming-the-impact-of-music-on-the-experience-of-compulsive-fantasizing.pdf)
- The SCID-interviewed sample of Somer et al. (2017) is described as "nearly half unemployed" with over 25% having attempted suicide. **[Clinical sample, secondary]** — [Soffer-Dudek & Somer 2018 intro (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5962718/)
- In the lockdown sample (N = 6,277), MD was linked to impairment in "social, family, and work-related activities." **[XS]** — [Musetti et al. 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.631979/full)
- In medical students: MD → procrastination r = .18, **GPA not affected** (N = 201). **[XS]** — [Karaağaç & Korkmaz 2025](https://www.turkpsikiyatri.com/Yayin/27th-National-Clinical-Education-Symposium-Presentation-Abstracts/maladaptive-daydreaming-and-academic-procrastination-the-relationship-with-psychological-symptoms-in-medical-students)
- In Sudanese medical students (N = 323, Arabic MDS), **34.3% self-identified** as MDers (mean MDS 33.6). This is a self-report screen; clinical interviews elsewhere confirm only about 60% of screen-positives. **[XS]** — [Middle East Current Psychiatry 2021 (DOI)](https://doi.org/10.1186/s43045-021-00122-8); [Soffer-Dudek & Theodor-Katz 2022 (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9091653/)
- Population prevalence: 4.2% of an Israeli community sample exceeded the MDS-16 cutoff; 5.5–8.5% of young adults and students did; an estimated **point prevalence of 2.5%** after clinical interviews (total N = 1,023). Rates are higher at younger ages. **[XS + interviews]** — [Soffer-Dudek & Theodor-Katz 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9091653/)

**Mood: depression, anxiety, dissociation, OCD (direction)**
- **Same day:** higher MD intensity went with higher OC symptoms (B = 1.79), dissociation (1.07), anxiety (0.59), negative emotion (0.57), social anxiety (0.34) and depression (0.33), and lower positive emotion (−0.43); all p ≤ .002. **[DIARY]** — [Soffer-Dudek & Somer 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5962718/)
- **Next day:** MD predicted more OC symptoms (1.04), dissociation (0.78) and negative emotion (0.37). It did **not** significantly predict next-day depression (0.10, p = .225) or anxiety (0.20, p = .082). The authors interpret dissociation as follows: hours of daydreaming "compromises the sense of presence in reality and brings about experiences of depersonalization and derealization." **[DIARY]** — [Soffer-Dudek & Somer 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5962718/)
- **13 months:** MD was highly stable (T1–T2 r = 0.68; standardized stability β = 0.70). MD at T1 predicted stress, anxiety and depression at T2. Standardized cross-lagged βs were small, about 0.07–0.11; for example, MD → depression β ≈ 0.08, 95% CI [0.011, 0.153]. Reverse paths were non-significant (β ≈ 0.01–0.04). The model explained 69.2% of variance in T2 depression. Conclusion: "MD is a stable condition and a **risk factor for an increase in psychological distress**." Mean MD decreased somewhat from T1 to T2. **[LONG, N = 814]** — [Musetti et al. 2023](https://air.unipr.it/retrieve/ee690cdb-2925-44d9-844e-fe5ae6f644c1/2023_Musetti%20et%20al.%20JBAdd.pdf)
- **Meta-analysis** (40 studies, N = 24,977): MD is positively associated with depression, anxiety, dissociation, OCD, ADHD, psychotic symptoms, ASD and trauma. Secondary associations include emotion-regulation difficulties, **loneliness**, negative affect, **shame**, somatic symptoms, **problematic internet use** and distress. MD is negatively associated with **self-efficacy and self-esteem**. Limitation: mostly cross-sectional self-report, so the authors "cannot definitively say whether imaginative addiction causes other mental health conditions or if prevailing distress triggers the need to escape into fantasy." **[META of XS]** — [Somer, Herscu, Samara & Abu-Rayya 2025, Int J Psychology (Kingston eprint)](https://eprints.kingston.ac.uk/56752); [PsyPost summary](https://www.psypost.org/excessive-daydreaming-is-strongly-linked-to-widespread-mental-health-disorders/)
- The comorbidity study found that **28.2% had attempted suicide** (N = 39 interviewed MDers). **[Clinical XS]** — [Somer, Soffer-Dudek & Ross 2017, J Nerv Ment Dis (PubMed)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28598955/)

**Sleep and bedtime daydreaming**
- MDers report that MD "interfered with their **sleep**" (Bigelsen & Schupak 2011). **[QUAL]** — [Somer et al. 2017](https://delphicentre.com.au/wp-content/uploads/2022/01/Somer_et_-al_Maladaptive-_Daydreaming.pdf)
- Lucas & Bone list "sleep and self-care neglect" among MD's secondary consequences. **[Clinical model]** — [Lucas & Bone 2025](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/the-cognitive-behaviour-therapist/article/introducing-a-psychological-formulation-model-of-maladaptive-daydreaming/883AC5B2DFE48DD252A2FA2EB6B82EE9)
- **Null daily-level result (pre-registered):** MD did **not** predict sleep disturbance the following night (B = −0.00, SE = 0.04, p = .956). Between persons, MD correlated with sleep disturbance at r = .23 (p = .008), but this became non-significant once mind-wandering was controlled. MD is highly trait-like: 75% of its variance was between persons. **[DIARY, N = 126]** — [Marcusson-Clavertz et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6880993/)

**Shame, secrecy and help-seeking**
- "Intense shame and exhaustive efforts to keep this behavior hidden from others, including mental health practitioners." **[QUAL]** — [Bigelsen & Schupak 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21959201/)
- Only 23% of the 90 had sought therapy, and dismissive responses from clinicians were a frequent complaint. **[QUAL]** — [Theodor-Katz et al. 2022 citing Bigelsen & Schupak](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9790222/)
- "Shame and concealment" was one of seven core themes. **[QUAL]** — [Somer, Somer & Jopp 2016b](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27002749/)

**Relationships, loneliness, self-esteem and identity**
- "Daydreaming and social isolation—a two-way street." **[QUAL]** — [Somer, Somer & Jopp 2016b](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27002749/)
- In 83–98 adults with ADHD, MD correlated with depression (r ≈ .36), loneliness (r ≈ .31) and low self-esteem (r ≈ .37). These correlations held after controlling for ADHD severity. The MD subgroup showed more depression (Hedges' g = 0.68 on one comparison), loneliness and lower self-esteem than ADHD-only participants. **[XS]** — [Theodor-Katz et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9790222/)
- During lockdown, probable MDers showed *decreased online* social relationships, read as a vicious cycle in which "withdrawal into an inner world… may have contributed to further estrangement." **[XS]** — [Musetti et al. 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.631979/full)
- Clinical consequences listed: "identity disruption and dissatisfaction with reality"; social withdrawal; shame, guilt and anxiety about the behavior. **[Clinical model]** — [Lucas & Bone 2025](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/the-cognitive-behaviour-therapist/article/introducing-a-psychological-formulation-model-of-maladaptive-daydreaming/883AC5B2DFE48DD252A2FA2EB6B82EE9)

### Inferences
**Cause → effect chains with evidence grade:**
- MD episode → hours lost (4.5 h/day; 57% vs. 16% of waking time) → less output. *[XS/DIARY]*
- MD episode → next-day ↑ negative emotion, ↑ dissociation (feeling unreal or "not present"), ↑ obsessive symptoms. *[DIARY: temporal order shown, not proven causation]*
- Chronic MD → ↑ stress, anxiety and depression over about a year (small effects). *[LONG: strongest directional evidence; reverse direction not supported]*
- MD → reduced external attention → inattention that can look like ADHD. *[Clinical self-report + XS]* General mind-wandering → worse task performance, worst on complex tasks. *[META]* Scripting, editing and business strategy are complex, demanding tasks, which is where off-task thought costs most.
- MD → shame/secrecy → isolation → loneliness → more need for compensatory fantasy. *[QUAL/XS; circular, direction unclear]*
- Bedtime MD → later sleep onset → poorer sleep is widely self-reported but **not confirmed** at the daily level. Sleep loss does increase next-day mind-wandering *[DIARY]*, which may still impair next-day focus.

### Gaps
- No study objectively measured work output (e.g., words written, hours of focused work) in MDers vs. controls. Academic outcome data are mixed: GPA was unaffected in one student sample.
- No longitudinal study of MD and attention or ADHD symptoms over time was found.
- Hours/day figures are self-estimates from self-selected online samples.
- The "nearly half unemployed" figure is a secondary description; the exact percentage in Somer et al. (2017) was not retrieved.

## 5. Success fantasies: Oettingen's research on positive fantasies vs. mental contrasting

### Takeaway
Across correlational, prospective and experimental studies, **indulging in idealized, effortless fantasies about a desired future predicts *less* effort and *worse* attainment**: fewer job applications and offers, lower salary, less weight lost, worse grades. Experimentally, such fantasies **lower energization**, measured by systolic blood pressure and felt energy, and reduce accomplishment over the following week. Over months, they predict **more depressive symptoms**, partly through poorer performance, even though they feel good in the moment. **Positive expectations** (judged likelihood of success) predict the opposite. **Mental contrasting**, which pairs the wish with the obstacles, plus implementation intentions (MCII/WOOP), improves goal attainment with a small-to-medium effect (g ≈ 0.34).

### Cited Findings
- Oettingen & Mayer (2002): "Positive expectations predicted high effort and successful performance, but the reverse was true for positive fantasies (experiencing one's thoughts and mental images about a desired future positively)." **[Prospective + EXP, multiple studies]** — [Oettingen & Mayer 2002, JPSP (DOI)](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.83.5.1198); [Semantic Scholar abstract](https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.1037/0022-3514.83.5.1198)
- In the job-seeker study, **83 German university students** rated how positive their fantasies about graduating and finding a job were. **Two years later**, those with more positive fantasies "put in **fewer job applications**, received **fewer job offers**, and ultimately earned **lower salaries**." **[Prospective]** — [NYU Stern / New Yorker summary](https://www.stern.nyu.edu/experience-stern/news/new-yorker-positive-thinking)
- The same pattern held for romantic crushes (more positive fantasies predicted a lower likelihood of forming a relationship over **5 months**), recovery from **hip surgery**, and **weight loss** (Oettingen & Wadden 1991). Positive fantasies predicted "indicators of low effort (e.g., few job applications submitted, few hours spent studying for an exam)." **[Prospective]** — [Kappes & Oettingen 2011, JESP (LSE repository)](https://researchonline.lse.ac.uk/id/eprint/46284)
- **Experimental mechanism, energy:** in Study 1, 164 female undergraduates fantasized positively vs. "questioningly" about wearing high-heeled shoes. Positive fantasy **lowered systolic blood pressure**: estimated change −1.73 mmHg vs. +0.23 mmHg, F(1,159) = 3.99, p < .05, η² = .024. In the positive condition, SBP fell significantly (t(82) = 2.33, p < .02); in the questioning condition it did not change. **[EXP]** — [Kappes & Oettingen 2011](https://researchonline.lse.ac.uk/id/eprint/46284)
- **Experimental mechanism, real-world accomplishment:** in Study 3, students who fantasized about an *ideal upcoming week* felt less energized immediately (M = 2.27 vs. 3.00, η² = .16) and reported **lower accomplishment a week later** (M = 5.74 vs. 6.57 on a 1–7 scale, η² = .10). Low energy **mediated** the effect (bootstrap indirect effect). Positive fantasies also had larger de-energizing effects when the need was pressing (Study 4). Authors: positive fantasies "allow people to **mentally consummate a desired future**," making effort seem unnecessary. **[EXP]** — [Kappes & Oettingen 2011](https://researchonline.lse.ac.uk/id/eprint/46284)
- **Depression over time:** "Positive fantasies about the future did indeed relate to **decreased symptoms of depression when measured concurrently**; however, positive fantasies predicted **more depressive symptoms when measured longitudinally**," for up to 7 months, in adults and schoolchildren. "In college students, **low academic success partially mediated** the predictive relation" (Study 4). **[Prospective, 4 studies]** — [Oettingen, Mayer & Portnow 2016, Psychological Science (PubMed)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26825106/)
- Study details: Study 1, 88 students, 1-month follow-up. Study 2, 109 fourth- and fifth-graders, 7 months. Study 3, 78 students sampled 8 times a day for 4 days, 6-month follow-up. Study 4, 148 students, where "positivity of fantasies predicted **lower course grades, which in turn predicted symptoms of depression**." Students with positive fantasies "tended to report **putting less effort into their coursework**." The authors stop short of claiming causation. **[Prospective]** — [APS press release](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/imagining-positive-outcomes-may-bring-pleasure-now-but-pain-later.html); [PsyPost](https://www.psypost.org/positive-fantasies-about-the-future-predict-symptoms-of-depression)
- **Mental contrasting with implementation intentions (MCII/WOOP):** a meta-analysis of 21 studies (24 effect sizes, 15,907 participants) found MCII effective for goal attainment, **g = 0.336**. Effects were stronger when delivered interactively with an experimenter (g = 0.465) than via documents (g = 0.277). Publication bias means the true effect may be smaller. **[META]** — [Wang, Wang & Gai 2021, Frontiers in Psychology (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8149892/)
- "Fantasies that are less positive – that question whether an ideal future can be achieved, and that depict obstacles, problems, and setbacks – should be more beneficial for mustering the energy needed to attain actual success." **[Authors' conclusion]** — [Kappes & Oettingen 2011 (summary)](https://researchonline.lse.ac.uk/id/eprint/46284)
- MD link: a participant in Somer's music study kept music *out* of her most precious success daydreams: "The daydreams I experience without music reflect my inner desires and medium to long-term goals… a successful career in science, a loving… wife, and happy children." Another reported success-performance fantasies: "when I am someone famous who answers a question in an interview in a very intelligent way… when I take an exam and prove a theorem." **[QUAL]** — [Somer 2024](https://www.somer.co.il/wp-content/uploads/2024/08/somer-2024-calling-the-tune-in-maladaptive-daydreaming-the-impact-of-music-on-the-experience-of-compulsive-fantasizing.pdf)

### Inferences
**Success-fantasy chain:** an exciting moment or an uncomfortable task → vivid fantasy of the successful business or life → feeling of having "mentally consummated" the goal → **lower energization** (lower SBP, less felt energy) → less effort (fewer applications, fewer study hours; for him, fewer scripts or edits shipped) → worse real outcomes → a widening gap between fantasy and reality → low mood (more depressive symptoms months later, mediated by poor performance) → more escape into the fantasy, which still feels good *in the moment*. This chain combines Oettingen's evidence (experimental for energy; prospective for outcomes and depression) with the MD "fantasy–reality gap" loop. Oettingen's samples were **not MDers**, so applying the findings to MD is an inference, though a close one: MD success fantasies are exactly the idealized, obstacle-free positive fantasies she studied.
- The counter-strategy has evidence: convert the fantasy into **mental contrasting** (wish → best outcome → **the inner obstacle**, e.g., "when the edit gets boring I reach for music" → if-then plan). The meta-analytic g ≈ 0.34 is modest but reliable.

### Gaps
- No study has tested Oettingen's paradigm on clinically diagnosed MDers or on success-themed MD specifically.
- The precise effect sizes from Oettingen & Mayer (2002), e.g. the salary difference, were not retrieved from the primary text; figures come from secondary summaries.

## 6. Excitement and positive arousal as a trigger

### Takeaway
There is **no direct study** of positive events or excitement as MD triggers. Indirect evidence supports the user's experience. Theory holds that **emotional arousal of either valence** ("affective salience") pulls thought toward fantasy. MDers score high on **reward responsiveness**. Heroic or upbeat music evokes exciting, motivating imagery. Oettingen's work shows that positive future fantasies feel like consumption of the reward. General mind-wandering studies, however, find that *negative* rather than positive mood increases off-task thought, so excitement-triggered MD is plausible but unproven.

### Cited Findings
- Affective salience, "the tendency of one aspect of experience to stand out due to its association with **emotional arousal**," is proposed as a primary automatic trigger of compulsive fantasy. "**Strong emotion** can exert a pull on thought. **The pleasure of emotional gratification** also commands cognitive resources in an automatic way." The definition covers arousal regardless of valence. **[Theory]** — [Burrell et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12562237/)
- Among 242 probable MDers, "**BAS Reward Responsiveness** subscale appeared to be most prevalent." The authors link MD to "motivational tendencies towards **reward seeking**." **[XS]** — [Wall & Clotworthy 2025](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/02762366251352852)
- Heroic-sounding music produced "more positive, **exciting**, constructive, and motivating thoughts" (Koelsch et al. 2019). An MDer: music "makes the daydream **more exciting** and adds emotion to the moment." **[EXP secondary + QUAL]** — [Somer 2024](https://www.somer.co.il/wp-content/uploads/2024/08/somer-2024-calling-the-tune-in-maladaptive-daydreaming-the-impact-of-music-on-the-experience-of-compulsive-fantasizing.pdf)
- In the general population, *negative* mood induction, relative to positive, increased lapses and task-irrelevant thought, while positive mood helped recovery after lapses. **[EXP]** — [Smallwood et al. 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19348539/)
- In MD diaries, *previous-day positive emotion* did not predict next-day MD. Same-day MD went with *lower* positive emotion (B = −0.43). **[DIARY]** — [Soffer-Dudek & Somer 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5962718/)
- Positive fantasies let people "mentally consummate a desired future," lowering energization, and this effect is **larger when the need is pressing** (Study 4). **[EXP]** — [Kappes & Oettingen 2011](https://researchonline.lse.ac.uk/id/eprint/46284)

### Inferences
- A plausible chain for the user: an exciting event (a good view count, a new client lead, an idea) → high arousal plus anticipated reward → affective salience pulls attention inward → a success fantasy elaborates the reward (with music raising the excitement) → he enjoys the "consumption" now → energy for the actual next step drops (Kappes & Oettingen). The first links rest on theory and trait data; the last is experimental, in non-MD samples.

### Gaps
- No experience-sampling or experimental study tests whether positive events or excitement precede MD episodes. This is an open question. The user's pattern is consistent with theory but is not directly evidenced.

## 7. Comorbidity and direction of effects (ADHD, anxiety, depression, OCD, dissociation, social anxiety)

### Takeaway
Comorbidity is very high in interviewed MDers: ADHD 76.9% (mostly inattentive), anxiety 71.8%, depression 66.7%, OCD 53.9%, and 74.4% had more than three additional disorders. The reverse rate is much lower: only about **20–23% of adults with ADHD** meet MD criteria. **Direction:** longitudinal and diary data point mainly to **MD → later distress** (depression, anxiety, stress, negative emotion, dissociation). The exception is **OCD, which runs both ways** as a daily "vicious cycle." For attention, MDers describe inattention as *secondary* to MD, and stimulants reportedly do not help MD. But no longitudinal MD–ADHD study exists, so "MD worsens attention", "attention problems lead to MD" and "both" all remain open.

### Cited Findings
- In 39 interviewed MDers: 74.4% met criteria for more than three additional disorders and 41.1% for more than four. **ADHD 76.9%**, anxiety disorder **71.8%**, depressive disorder **66.7%**, OCD or related disorder **53.9%**; **28.2%** had attempted suicide. **[Clinical XS]** — [Somer, Soffer-Dudek & Ross 2017 (PubMed)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28598955/)
- The ADHD cases were "mostly of the inattentive type." **[Clinical XS]** — [Theodor-Katz et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9790222/)
- Reverse direction: of 83 adults with diagnosed ADHD, **20.5%** met MD criteria (an estimated 23.3% after accounting for dropouts). The authors suggest that "the attention deficit observed in those cases with MD may be **a consequence of their compulsion to daydream**," and that "if MD were included in the DSM, the diagnosis of ADHD might have been superfluous in certain cases." Limitation: "cross-sectional design that does not allow inferences about directionality." **[XS + interviews]** — [Theodor-Katz et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9790222/)
- Medication: in 202 MDers (Ross et al. 2019), "stimulants were reported to have **next to no positive effect** on participants' daydreaming patterns" and "were also reported to **increase MD symptoms**." **[Self-report survey, secondary]** — [Theodor-Katz et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9790222/)
- In the general population, "people with fewer cognitive resources tend to engage in more mind-wandering." This supports an "attention capacity → more off-task thought" direction for ordinary mind-wandering. **[META]** — [Randall et al. 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25089941/)
- The criteria developers' view: "a simple primary–secondary relationship between MD and other disorders does not seem to exist." MD "overlaps with, and co-occurs with" ADHD, OCD-spectrum disorders and major depression. **[Expert opinion]** — [Somer et al. 2017](https://delphicentre.com.au/wp-content/uploads/2022/01/Somer_et_-al_Maladaptive-_Daydreaming.pdf)
- **OCD runs both ways (daily):** OC symptoms predicted next-day MD (B = 1.30), and MD predicted next-day OC symptoms (B = 1.04); "MD and obsessive-compulsive symptoms coincided in what seems to be a **vicious cycle**." **[DIARY]** — [Soffer-Dudek & Somer 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5962718/)
- **Distress mainly follows MD (13 months):** MD → stress, anxiety and depression were significant; distress → MD was not. **[LONG]** — [Musetti et al. 2023](https://air.unipr.it/retrieve/ee690cdb-2925-44d9-844e-fe5ae6f644c1/2023_Musetti%20et%20al.%20JBAdd.pdf)
- **Dissociation:** increased depersonalization and derealization were "reported on days of increased daydreaming and the following day." **[DIARY, via position paper]** — [Soffer-Dudek et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12038384/)
- In 100 dissociative-disorder inpatients, 49 met criteria for MD disorder and 23 for unspecified MD. **[Clinical XS]** — [Ross, Ridgway & George 2020 (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9176046/)
- **Social anxiety and trauma as antecedents:** "Traumatic experiences in childhood and social anxiety have been identified as potential predictor[s] for the development of compulsive fantasy (Somer & Herscu, 2017)." **[XS mediation, secondary]** — [Burrell et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12562237/)
- Social anxiety rose with MD on the same day (B = 0.34, p = .002) but was not tested as a significant next-day predictor. **[DIARY]** — [Soffer-Dudek & Somer 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5962718/)
- The 2025 meta-analysis (40 studies, N = 24,977) confirms positive associations with depression, anxiety, dissociation, OCD, ADHD and others, mostly from cross-sectional data. **[META]** — [Kingston eprint](https://eprints.kingston.ac.uk/56752)

### Inferences
- For attention, a **bidirectional model** is most consistent with the data. A lower baseline attention capacity makes off-task thought easier (general META). MD then trains habitual inward attention, so MDers experience their inattention as caused by MD (clinical self-report), and stimulants do not fix it. Neither direction has been tested longitudinally in MD.
- For mood, the strongest available evidence (diary plus 13-month cross-lagged data) runs **MD → worse mood and distress**. That does not rule out fast, within-day "distress → MD" effects (Section 3).

### Gaps
- No longitudinal study of MD and ADHD or inattention symptoms.
- No MD-specific data for **Muslim or Arab male** populations beyond prevalence screens, such as the Sudan medical-student study.
- The comorbidity percentages come from a single small interviewed sample (N = 39), recruited online.

## 8. Internet and media use and MD (social media, short-form video, gaming)

### Takeaway
MD is consistently associated with **problematic internet use**, most strongly **problematic social media use (PSMU)** and, in one study, **gaming addiction**. The usual explanation is a shared function: escape and compensatory regulation, plus curating an idealized self on social media. All quantitative evidence is cross-sectional. I found no study of **short-form video** specifically.

### Cited Findings
- In a network analysis (N = 1,209 Italians, aged 18–73), MD's strongest conditional links were with **PSMU (partial r = 0.25)** and cyberchondria (0.23). Links with problematic online gaming (0.09), cybersex (0.08) and online shopping (0.05) were weak, and there was **no link with online gambling**. "The cross-sectional design precludes examining the directionality." **[XS]** — [Pezzi et al. 2024, J Psychiatric Research (PDF)](https://art.torvergata.it/retrieve/0ffe267e-94d2-434c-b9e0-9f4f848cffc0/Pezzi%20et%20al.%2c%202024.pdf)
- Interpretation in the same paper: both MD and PSMU "might serve as maladaptive coping strategies for temporary relief from emotional distress." Because MD fantasies often involve "idealized characteristics and are admired by others," "MD might increase the risk for PSMU, as social media might allow individuals to manage their own identity in accordance with an **idealized self**." MD mediated the link between anxious attachment styles and PSMU (Costanzo et al. 2021). MDers with high MD reported higher PSMU (Chirico et al. 2024). **[XS, review]** — [Pezzi et al. 2024](https://art.torvergata.it/retrieve/0ffe267e-94d2-434c-b9e0-9f4f848cffc0/Pezzi%20et%20al.%2c%202024.pdf)
- In 293 Turkish medical students, probable MDers had more severe **gaming addiction** (p = .03), **problematic social media use** (p = .01), food addiction, sex/pornography addiction and compulsive buying than non-MDers. "Only **gaming addiction** was a significant statistical predictor of MD." **[XS]** — [Health Sciences Quarterly 2024 (DOI 10.26900/hsq.2395)](https://avesis.usak.edu.tr/yayin/88df7de0-8afe-4e91-a272-a91c798daafc/association-of-maladaptive-daydreaming-with-behavioral-addiction)
- In 437 Hungarian adolescents and adults (78.3% male), celebrity worship was associated with **problematic internet use, maladaptive daydreaming, and desire for fame**. **[XS]** — [Zsila, McCutcheon & Demetrovics 2018, J Behav Addict (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6426373/)
- Case evidence: MD "was coupled with **excessive Internet use and viewing porn**." **[QUAL, case]** — [Pietkiewicz et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6426361/)
- Case evidence: an adolescent used role-playing games "to re-enact traumatic situations… and co-create alternative fantasy scenarios"; a 25-year-old male used the internet for escapism in a loop of impairment → MD → more internet use. **[QUAL, secondary]** — [Pezzi et al. 2024](https://art.torvergata.it/retrieve/0ffe267e-94d2-434c-b9e0-9f4f848cffc0/Pezzi%20et%20al.%2c%202024.pdf)
- During lockdown, probable MDers reported **decreased online** social relationships. MD is not simply "more social media"; it can involve withdrawal from social contact online too. **[XS]** — [Musetti et al. 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.631979/full)
- The meta-analysis lists **problematic internet use** among MD's secondary correlates. **[META]** — [Kingston eprint](https://eprints.kingston.ac.uk/56752)

### Inferences
- For a content creator, the most relevant link is **social media → comparison and idealized-self content → material for success fantasies**, and **MD ↔ PSMU** as twin escape behaviors. Both fit the same aversive-task → escape loop. Cross-sectional data cannot say which comes first.

### Gaps
- No studies were found on **short-form video** (TikTok, Reels, Shorts) and MD, nor on YouTube consumption specifically.
- No longitudinal or experimental data on internet use and MD direction.
- Web search budget ran out before I could look for 2025–2026 studies on MD and short-form video; this area may have newer work not captured here.
