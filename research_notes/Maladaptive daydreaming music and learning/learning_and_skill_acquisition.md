# Learning and Skill Acquisition: What Works for Learning Fast and Getting Good (for an adult self-learner / creator-entrepreneur)

Evidence labels used throughout:
- **[META]** peer-reviewed meta-analysis or quantitative synthesis
- **[REVIEW]** peer-reviewed narrative/expert review or monograph
- **[PRIMARY]** peer-reviewed primary experiment/study
- **[POPULAR]** popular-science book (not peer-reviewed; synthesizes research plus anecdote)
- **[PRACTITIONER]** practitioner writing / blog / newsletter (not peer-reviewed)
- **[SECONDARY]** press release, news summary, or blog summary of a study (used where the primary text could not be fetched)

Effect-size rule of thumb used below: Cohen's d / Hedges' g of ~0.2 = small, ~0.5 = medium, ~0.8 = large; correlation r of ~0.1 small, ~0.3 medium, ~0.5 large.

## A1. Which learning techniques have the strongest evidence, and how big are the effects?

### Takeaway
Retrieval practice (self-testing) and spacing are the best-supported techniques, with medium effects (g ≈ 0.5) that get larger when combined (spaced retrieval g ≈ 0.74). Self-explanation (g ≈ 0.55), generation (d ≈ 0.40), interleaving (g ≈ 0.42, but highly variable), worked examples for novices, and combining words with pictures have moderate support. Rereading, highlighting and summarizing are rated low-utility even though they are what most learners do.

### Cited Findings

**Overall ranking (Dunlosky et al. 2013)**
- [REVIEW] Dunlosky, Rawson, Marsh, Nathan & Willingham (2013, *Psychological Science in the Public Interest* 14(1)) rated 10 techniques against learning conditions, student characteristics, materials and criterion tasks. **High utility:** practice testing, distributed practice. **Moderate:** elaborative interrogation, self-explanation, interleaved practice. **Low:** summarization, highlighting/underlining, keyword mnemonic, imagery for text, rereading. — [APS / PSPI](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/pspi/1529100612453266/); [ScienceDaily press release](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2013/01/130110111734.htm); [Scott Barry Kaufman summary](https://scottbarrykaufman.com/study-alert-improving-students-learning-with-effective-learning-techniques-from-cognitive-and-educational-psychology)
- [REVIEW] The moderate techniques fell short of "high" because evidence was limited (e.g., elaborative interrogation and self-explanation not adequately tested in educational settings; interleaving only beginning to be explored at the time). Summarization and imagery help "some students on some criterion tasks" under limited conditions. — [Dunlosky et al. 2013 summary](https://scottbarrykaufman.com/study-alert-improving-students-learning-with-effective-learning-techniques-from-cognitive-and-educational-psychology)

**Retrieval practice / testing effect**
- [META] Rowland (2014, *Psychological Bulletin* 140(6)): 159 effect sizes from 61 studies (1975–2013); testing vs. restudy **g = 0.50**. Recall-type initial tests produced larger benefits than recognition tests, supporting "effortful processing" as a mechanism. — [Rowland 2014 PDF](https://courseware.epfl.ch/assets/courseware/v1/fdde2f0aa590bf3b1324077a6bf1540c/asset-v1%3AEPFL%2BDEMO%2B2020%2Btype%40asset%2Bblock/Rowland2014-meta-analysis.pdf)
- [META] Adesope, Trevisan & Sundararajan (2017, *Review of Educational Research* 87(3)): 118 articles, 272 independent effect sizes, 15,472 participants. Practice tests beat restudying **and all other comparison conditions**; effects moderated by test features, participant characteristics and study design. — [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.3102/0034654316689306); [WSU repository](https://rex.libraries.wsu.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/Rethinking-the-Use-of-Tests-A/99900601154801842)
- [SECONDARY] Learning Scientists summary of Adesope et al.: retrieval practice consistently better than restudy; "hybrid" (mixed-format) practice tests most effective. — [Learning Scientists blog](https://www.learningscientists.org/blog/2017/2/9-1)
- [META] Yang, Luo, Vadillo, Yu & Shanks (2021, *Psychological Bulletin* 147(4)): **classroom** studies, 222 independent studies, 48,478 students; quizzing raised achievement **g = 0.499**. Moderators: what the control group did, format match between quiz and final test, material match, **corrective feedback**, number of test repetitions, timing, treatment duration. — [HKBU record](https://scholars.hkbu.edu.hk/en/publications/testing-quizzing-boosts-classroom-learning-a-systematic-and-meta-/); [PDF](https://gwern.net/docs/spacedrepetition/2021-yang.pdf)

**Spacing (distributed practice)**
- [META] Cepeda, Pashler, Vul, Wixted & Rohrer (2006, *Psychological Bulletin* 132): 839 assessments in 317 experiments from 184 articles. The gap between study sessions (ISI) and the retention interval interact: **the optimal gap grows as the time you need to remember it grows**. — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16719566/); [PDF](https://www.evullab.org/pdf/CepedaPashlerVulWixtedRohrer-PB-2006.pdf)
- [PRIMARY] Cepeda et al. (2008, *Psychological Science* 19(11)): 1,350+ participants, study gaps up to 3.5 months, final tests up to 1 year later. Optimal gap ≈ **20–40% of the retention interval for a 1-week test, falling to ≈5–10% for a 1-year test**. The authors conclude many educational practices are "highly inefficient." — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19076480/); [PDF](https://www.evullab.org/pdf/CepedaVulRohrerWixtedPashler-PS-2008.pdf)
- [META] Latimier, Peyre & Ramus (2021, *Educational Psychology Review*): 29 studies, 39 effect sizes; **spaced retrieval vs. massed retrieval g = 0.74**; combining spacing + retrieval beats either alone; **no clear evidence that expanding intervals beat uniform intervals**. — [Tips for Teachers summary](https://tipsforteachers.substack.com/p/research-bite-53-a-meta-analytic); [ACU summary](https://inspire.acu.edu.au/articles/spaced-practice)

**Interleaving**
- [META] Brunmair & Richter (2019, *Psychological Bulletin*, "Similarity matters"): 59 studies, 238 effect sizes, 158 samples; overall **g = 0.42**, but **substantial heterogeneity**: interleaving beats blocking "in only some learning situations." Strongest for paintings and other visual materials. — [Author PDF](https://www.psychologie.uni-wuerzburg.de/fileadmin/06020400/2019/Brunmair_Richter_in_press__2019_META-ANALYSIS_OF_INTERLEAVED_LEARNING.pdf); DOI 10.1037/bul0000209

**Elaboration, self-explanation, generation**
- [META] Bisra, Liu, Nesbit, Salimi & Winne (2018, *Educational Psychology Review*): 64 reports, 69 effect sizes, ~5,917 learners; prompting self-explanation **g = 0.55**. Worked for conceptual and procedural knowledge across most subjects, and **survived controls for time on task**. — [BPS Research Digest](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/self-explanation-powerful-learning-technique-according-meta-analysis-64-studies); [PDF](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/spaced-repetition/2018-bisra.pdf)
- [META] Bertsch, Pesta, Wiscott & McDaniel (2007, *Memory & Cognition*): 445 effect sizes from 86 studies; **generation effect ≈ 0.40 SD** (producing information beats reading it), with large variation by moderator. — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17645161/)

**Worked examples, cognitive load, expertise reversal**
- [REVIEW] Kalyuga, Ayres, Chandler & Sweller (2003, *Educational Psychologist* 38(1)), "The expertise reversal effect": working memory is severely limited; schemas in long-term memory get around that limit. **Worked examples beat problem-solving for novices, but the advantage disappears and can reverse as expertise grows.** Instructional aids that help novices (e.g., integrated text plus diagram) become redundant for more experienced learners, who did better with the diagram alone. — [UOW record](https://ro.uow.edu.au/edupapers/136); [Wikipedia overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expertise_reversal_effect)

**Dual coding / multimedia**
- [REVIEW] Mayer's multimedia principle: people learn better from words plus pictures than from words alone. In Mayer's experimental comparisons on transfer tests, median effect sizes were about **d = 1.39** (11 comparisons) and d = 1.50 (9 of 9 studies), with d = 1.35 in later work. These are mostly lab studies with short science explanations. Builds on Paivio's dual-coding theory (separate verbal and pictorial systems). — [Mayer, research-based principles PDF (UNH)](https://www.unh.edu/teaching-learning-resource-hub/sites/default/files/media/2023-06/itow-research-based-principles-for-designing-multimedia-instruction-mayer.pdf); [Cambridge Handbook of Multimedia Learning excerpt](https://assets.cambridge.org/052183/8738/excerpt/0521838738_excerpt.htm)

### Inferences
- Retrieval practice is the best "default" technique. For skills like scriptwriting, editing or prompting, the skill version of retrieval is **producing from memory**: writing a hook or rebuilding an edit without looking at the reference, then comparing. A flashcard is the factual version.
- The spacing data imply the review gap should scale with how long the skill or knowledge must last. For knowledge meant to last a year, revisit roughly every month or so (5–10% of a year). For something needed next week, revisit after about a day or two.
- The expertise reversal effect means a creator should study complete expert examples (full scripts, full edits, full sales pages) early on, then shift to producing on their own as competence grows. Continuing to consume worked examples as an intermediate becomes redundant and can hurt.
- Interleaving is most useful for learning to discriminate between things that look alike, such as hook types, title formulas, editing styles, or thumbnail compositions. Mixing examples of each, rather than studying one type in a block, should sharpen the ability to tell which one fits.

### Gaps
- Adesope et al.'s pooled overall g and its sub-moderator values could not be retrieved (the WSU PDF link was dead and the blog summary gave no numbers). It is often quoted as ~0.6, but that figure is **unverified here**.
- I found no meta-analysis isolating elaborative interrogation's effect size in this session.
- Nearly all technique research uses verbal or declarative material or math problems. Direct evidence for creative or production skills (editing, scriptwriting) is extrapolation.

## A2. Desirable difficulties, illusions of competence, why learners pick bad strategies, and metacognition

### Takeaway
How easy learning feels is a poor guide to how much was learned. Conditions that speed up short-term performance (massing, blocking, rereading, fluent lectures) often weaken long-term retention and transfer, and learners consistently prefer them anyway. Effortful conditions (spacing, interleaving, testing, generation, varying practice) help durable learning, but only if the learner can still succeed at them.

### Cited Findings
- [REVIEW] Bjork & Bjork (2011), "Making things hard on yourself, but in a good way": **storage strength** (how entrenched or interconnected a memory is) vs. **retrieval strength** (how accessible it is right now). "The conditions that most rapidly increase retrieval strength differ from the conditions that maximize the gain of storage strength." Learners who mistake current retrieval strength for storage strength come to prefer poorer learning conditions. Desirable difficulties: varying conditions, interleaving, spacing, testing, generation. **Caveat:** difficulties are desirable only if the learner can respond to them successfully. — [Bjork & Bjork 2011 PDF](https://bjorklab.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/13/2016/04/EBjork_RBjork_2011.pdf); [UNH handout](https://www.unh.edu/teaching-learning-resource-hub/sites/default/files/media/2023-06/itow-introducing-desirable-difficulties-into-practice-and-instruction-bjork-and-bjork.pdf)
- [PRIMARY] Kornell & Bjork (2008, *Psychological Science*): learning artists' styles from paintings. Interleaved ("spaced") presentation improved classification of **new** paintings, yet participants rated massed study as more effective **even after their own test results showed the opposite**. Secondary summaries report ~78% judged massing as better or equal. I could not verify the exact wording from the PDF. — [Kornell & Bjork 2008 PDF](https://bjorklab.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/13/2016/07/Kornell_Bjork_2008_PsychScience.pdf); [Psychology in Action summary](https://www.psychologyinaction.org/?p=820)
- [PRIMARY] Karpicke, Butler & Roediger (2009, *Memory*): asked open-endedly about their study strategies, college students most often reported **rereading** notes or textbooks. Few used self-testing as a learning strategy. — [Karpicke et al. 2009 PDF](https://bpb-us-e2.wpmucdn.com/sites.wustl.edu/dist/8/805/files/2026/06/Karpicke-et-al.-2009-Metacognitive-strategies-in-student-learning-Do-students-practise-retrieval-when-they-study-on-thei.pdf)
- [PRIMARY] Carpenter, Wilford, Kornell & Mullaney (2013, *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review*): students watched a 22-minute video lecture given fluently or disfluently by the same instructor. The fluent version produced higher instructor ratings and higher confidence, but **test scores did not consistently differ**. "Instructor fluency increases perceptions of learning without increasing actual learning." — [Royal Holloway record](https://pure.royalholloway.ac.uk/en/publications/the-effect-of-instructor-fluency-on-students-perceptions-of-instr/)
- [PRIMARY] Deslauriers, McCarty, Miller, Callaghan & Kestin (2019, *PNAS* 116(39)): in college physics, students learned **more** from active instruction but **felt** they learned less than students in polished lectures from highly rated instructors. The extra cognitive effort was read as a sign of poorer learning. — [PDF](https://openlab.citytech.cuny.edu/gamelab/files/2023/01/Deslauriersa-et-al-2019.pdf); [UNL news](https://newsroom.unl.edu/announce/csmce/10018/58996)
- [PRIMARY] Kardas & O'Brien (2018, *Psychological Science*): 6 experiments, 2,225 participants. **Repeatedly watching others perform** (dart throwing, the moonwalk, an online game) raised people's belief that they could do it, but their actual ability did not improve. Watching shows *what steps to take* but not *how they feel*; a brief "taste" of trying it (e.g., holding the juggling pins) reduced the illusion. — [PDF](https://gwern.net/doc/psychology/cognitive-bias/illusion-of-depth/2018-kardas.pdf); [Chicago Booth press release](https://www.chicagobooth.edu/media-relations-and-communications/press-releases/easier-seen-than-done-watching-how-to-videos-on-youtube-gives-people-false-sense-of-expertise)

### Inferences
- A practical calibration rule: **"if it feels smooth, it's probably not sticking."** The learner should judge progress by unaided output (can I write a 30-second hook cold? can I rebuild this transition without the tutorial?), not by a sense of familiarity.
- The "feel" of learning should be deliberately ignored when choosing a method. Keeping a simple log that predicts performance before a self-test and records the actual score afterward trains calibration. This is an inference from the calibration and fluency findings, not a tested protocol.
- Difficulty must stay "desirable." When attempts mostly fail, step back to worked examples (see the expertise reversal effect in A1).

### Gaps
- I found no meta-analysis quantifying how much metacognitive or calibration training itself improves self-learners' outcomes. This area was not searched in depth.

## A3. Learning myths: learning styles, "10% of the brain", multitasking

### Takeaway
Matching instruction to a "learning style" has no supporting evidence. The "10% of the brain" claim is false. Doing two attention-demanding tasks at once is really fast switching, with measurable costs to speed, accuracy and learning.

### Cited Findings
- [REVIEW] Pashler, McDaniel, Rohrer & Bjork (2008, *Psychological Science in the Public Interest*). The "meshing hypothesis" holds that instruction matched to a learner's preferred modality works better. Testing it properly requires classifying learners by style, randomly assigning each style group to different instruction methods, and giving everyone the same test. The few adequately designed studies **contradicted** meshing. "If classification of students' learning styles has practical utility, it remains to be demonstrated." — [USF repository](https://digitalcommons.usf.edu/psy_facpub/1765/); [Deans for Impact summary](https://deansforimpact.org/learning-styles-what-does-the-research-say/)
- [PRIMARY] Dekker, Lee, Howard-Jones & Jolles (2012, *Frontiers in Psychology*): a survey of 242 UK and Dutch teachers interested in neuroscience found neuromyths, including learning styles, widely endorsed. — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3475349/)
- [SECONDARY/expert column] "10% of the brain": brain imaging (EEG, MEG, PET, fMRI) has localized functions throughout the brain, and "no quiet areas awaiting new assignments have emerged" (Barry Beyerstein, *Scientific American*). The myth spread through self-help writing, e.g., Lowell Thomas's 1936 preface to Carnegie's *How to Win Friends and Influence People*. — [Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/do-people-only-use-10-percent-of-their-brains/); [Wikipedia overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ten-percent-of-the-brain_myth)
- [PRIMARY] Rubinstein, Meyer & Evans (2001, *J. Exp. Psych: Human Perception & Performance*), four experiments: task switching involves distinct executive stages of "goal shifting" and "rule activation," and each switch adds time. The often-quoted "up to 40% of productive time" is **Meyer's extrapolation**, not a number reported in the paper. — [Newswise/APA release](https://www.newswise.com/articles/is-multitasking-more-efficient); [Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/multitasking-may-diminish)
- [PRIMARY] Sana, Weston & Cepeda (2013, *Computers & Education*): in simulated lectures, students who multitasked on laptops scored **11% lower** on an immediate comprehension test, and non-multitasking students **in view of** a multitasking peer scored **17% lower**. — [York U news](https://news.yorku.ca/2013/03/13/multitasking-on-laptop-impedes-classroom-learning-york-u-study-shows/); [Cepeda FAQ](https://www.yorku.ca/ncepeda/laptopFAQ.html)

### Inferences
- The user should not pick "visual" vs. "auditory" formats based on a learning-style label. Choose the format that suits the *content* (editing is visual and hands-on; scriptwriting is verbal), and combine words with visuals where that helps (A1).
- "Learning while multitasking" (a tutorial playing while scrolling, or music videos plus study) should be treated as lowering learning, not as efficiency.

### Gaps
- I did not retrieve exact prevalence percentages from Dekker et al. 2012, so none are quoted.

## A4. Sleep and exercise (brief, practical)

### Takeaway
Sleep before learning protects the ability to encode new memories, and sleep after learning helps consolidate them. Short bouts of aerobic exercise give small to moderate memory benefits, mainly when they fall around the consolidation window after learning.

### Cited Findings
- [PRIMARY] Yoo, Hu, Gujar, Jolesz & Walker (2007, *Nature Neuroscience*): one night of sleep deprivation produced about a **40% reduction** in the ability to form new memories (encoding), with a selective deficit in the bilateral posterior hippocampus. — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17293859/); [Harvard Gazette](https://news.harvard.edu/gazette/story/2007/02/study-shows-importance-of-sleep-for-optimal-memory-functioning)
- [REVIEW] Diekelmann, Wilhelm & Born (2009), "The whats and whens of sleep-dependent memory consolidation": sleep after learning enhances consolidation of declarative, procedural and emotional memories. The benefit is **greater for weakly than strongly encoded** material and more consistent for explicitly encoded memories. Mechanism: reactivation redistributes memories from hippocampus to cortex ("active system consolidation"). — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2739268); [Lübeck record](https://research.uni-luebeck.de/de/publications/the-whats-and-whens-of-sleep-dependent-memory-consolidation/)
- [META] Roig, Nordbrandt, Geertsen & Nielsen (2013): acute exercise improved short-term/working memory (**SMD = 0.26**) and long-term memory (**SMD = 0.52**). Working-memory benefits were larger with shorter (<20 min), lower-intensity exercise. — [Loprinzi et al. 2019 citing Roig, PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6523402/)
- [META] Loprinzi et al. (2019, 25 articles): exercise **before encoding** d = 0.11 (not significant); during **early consolidation** d = 0.47; during **late consolidation** d = 1.05 (wide CI). — [PMC6523402](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6523402/)

### Inferences
- Practical rules: protect sleep the night **before** a learning or creative block, since sleep loss hits encoding, and the night **after**, for consolidation. A walk or light cardio after a study session is a low-cost, plausibly helpful habit.
- Night-time daydreaming that delays sleep would cost learning through the encoding and consolidation pathways. That link is an inference; how maladaptive daydreaming relates to sleep was not researched here.

### Gaps
- No dose-response guidance (e.g., minimum sleep hours for learning benefits) was collected. Exercise meta-analyses vary widely in protocols, and the late-consolidation effect has a very wide confidence interval.

## A5. Attention and learning: mind-wandering, media multitasking, attention residue, task switching, deep work

### Takeaway
Mind-wandering during learning reliably goes with worse comprehension (r ≈ −0.2). Building retrieval checkpoints into video learning roughly **halves** mind-wandering and improves retention. Evidence that habitual media multitasking or heavy short-form video use damages attention is mostly correlational and sometimes fails to replicate, but it points consistently in a negative direction. Switching tasks leaves "attention residue" that hurts the next task.

### Cited Findings

**Mind-wandering**
- [META] Randall, Oswald & Beier (2014, *Psychological Bulletin* 140(6)): theory-driven meta-analysis of mind-wandering and performance across cognitive tasks. The overall association is **about r = −0.24**, as cited by Bonifacci et al. 2022. — [Bonifacci et al. 2022, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9971160/); [NYU bibliography listing](https://ifp.nyu.edu/2025/meta-analyses-systematic-reviews/bul0000424/)
- [META] Bonifacci, Viroli, Vassura, Colombini & Desideri (2022/2023): 25 papers, 73 correlations, 3,926 participants. Mind-wandering vs. **reading comprehension r = −0.21** (95% CI −0.24 to −0.18). With trait questionnaires instead of in-the-moment probes, the correlation rose by ~0.305, to almost zero. In other words, **momentary** mind-wandering during reading, not trait tendency, predicts poorer comprehension. Text difficulty, interest, working memory, text type and length were non-significant moderators. — [PMC9971160](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9971160/)
- [PRIMARY] Szpunar, Khan & Schacter (2013, *PNAS* 110(16)), Experiment 2, a 21-minute statistics video lecture in four ~5.5-minute segments:
  - **Mind-wandering** (share of probes answered "yes"): 19% with brief tests after each segment, vs. 39% for restudy and 41% with no tests.
  - **Extra note-taking:** 24% vs. 9% vs. 7% of slides.
  - **Final cumulative test:** 90% vs. 76% vs. 68%.
  - Testing also reduced anxiety about the final test.
  
  — [PMC3631699](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3631699/)

**Media multitasking**
- [PRIMARY] Ophir, Nass & Wagner (2009, *PNAS*): 262 students surveyed; the 19 heaviest and 22 lightest media multitaskers were tested. **Heavy media multitaskers were worse at filtering irrelevant information and slower at task switching**, e.g., ~77 ms slower with distractors present. — [PMC2747151](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2747151); [Stanford News](https://news.stanford.edu/stories/2009/08/multitask-research-study-082409?view=print)
- [META/REPLICATION] Wiradhany & Nieuwenstein (2017): direct replications of Ophir were **inconclusive** (Bayes factors mostly indecisive, though in the same direction). Their meta-analysis of ~40 studies found most later studies report **small and non-significant** effects. — [PMC5662702](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5662702)
- [REVIEW] Uncapher & Wagner (2018, *PNAS*), reviewing 21 reports: "the balance of evidence suggests that heavier media multitaskers exhibit poorer performance in a number of cognitive domains," while the literature remains sparse and mixed. — [PMC5662702 discussion](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5662702); [Psychonomic Society blog](https://featuredcontent.psychonomic.org/the-good-the-bad-and-the-media-multi-tasking-its-all-the-internets-fault-or-is-it/)
- [META] Nguyen et al. (2025, *Psychological Bulletin*): 71 studies, 98,299 participants. Heavier **short-form video** use (TikTok, Reels, Shorts) was associated with poorer cognition overall (**r = −0.34**), with the strongest links for **attention (r = −0.38)** and **inhibitory control (r = −0.41)**. It was also associated with more anxiety, stress and depression. **Correlational**: direction of causation not established. — [PsyPost summary](https://www.psypost.org/large-meta-analysis-links-tiktok-and-instagram-reels-to-poorer-cognitive-and-mental-health/); [NYU abstract listing](https://ifp.nyu.edu/2025/journal-article-abstracts/bul0000498/)
- [PRIMARY + META] Ward et al. (2017), "brain drain": the mere presence of one's smartphone reduced available working memory. Direct replications **failed** (FORRT replication atlas records 2 failed replications). A later meta-analysis (22 studies) concluded that smartphone presence does affect cognition, more so for memory and attention than for simple tasks. — [FORRT atlas](https://forrt.org/flora-replication-atlas/doi/10.1086/691462/); [Meta-analysis, PMC10525686](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10525686/); [BPS digest](https://www.bps.org.uk/research-digest/revisiting-brain-drain-effect)

**Attention residue / task switching**
- [PRIMARY] Leroy (2009, *Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes*), "Why is it so hard to do my work?": after switching away from an **unfinished** task, attention residue from it lowers performance on the next task. **Finishing the first task, especially under time pressure, creates cognitive closure** and reduces residue. — [strategy+business summary](https://strategy-business.com/article/re00088); [UW Bothell news](https://www.uwb.edu/news/?p=30821)
- [SECONDARY] The claim that switching adds "25% to well over 100%" in time for complex tasks comes from a strategy+business write-up, and **its original source is unclear**. Treat it as illustrative only. — [strategy+business](https://strategy-business.com/article/re00088)
- [POPULAR] Cal Newport's *Deep Work* (2016) popularized attention residue and long, undistracted focus blocks. It is a popular book, not empirical evidence; I did not retrieve a source in this session.

### Inferences
- For someone prone to heavy daydreaming, the strongest evidence-based lever is to **make learning active and segmented**: ~5–10-minute input chunks, each followed by a retrieval prompt (close the video and write/say/do what you just saw). Szpunar's design halved mind-wandering in exactly this format (video lectures).
- Momentary mind-wandering, not trait tendency, predicts comprehension loss. So environment and task design (shorter segments, frequent output, no phone in view) can matter a lot even for someone with a strong daydreaming tendency.
- Short-form video bingeing is plausibly bad for this user's goals because of its attention and inhibitory-control correlations, but the evidence is correlational. A reasonable stance: treat Shorts and Reels as research *material* consumed deliberately, not as ambient filler.
- Finishing small units of work before switching (Leroy) argues for batching: e.g., finish the script's hook section before opening the editor.

### Gaps
- Maladaptive daydreaming specifically, as distinct from ordinary task-unrelated thought, was **not** covered in these sources. Whether these findings carry over to MD is an inference (other notes in this project cover MD).
- I did not verify the effect sizes in the 2023 brain-drain meta-analysis. Randall et al.'s moderator results (e.g., task complexity) were not retrieved directly.

## A6. Learning from video and online content: passive consumption vs. active learning

### Takeaway
Watching tutorials and polished explanations reliably raises *confidence* more than *competence*. Active learning (doing, answering, producing) produces better results even though it feels worse.

### Cited Findings
- [META] Freeman et al. (2014, *PNAS*): 225 studies of undergraduate STEM. Active learning raised exam scores by **~6%**, and students in traditional lectures were **1.5× more likely to fail**. Effects held across disciplines and class sizes (largest in small classes, n ≤ 50), with larger gains on concept inventories than on exams. Publication-bias checks suggest the result is robust. — [PMC4060683](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4060683)
- [PRIMARY] Deslauriers et al. (2019, *PNAS*): active learning produced more learning but a lower *feeling* of learning (see A2). — [PDF](https://openlab.citytech.cuny.edu/gamelab/files/2023/01/Deslauriersa-et-al-2019.pdf)
- [PRIMARY] Kardas & O'Brien (2018): merely watching others creates an "illusion of skill acquisition"; trying it yourself briefly reduces the illusion (see A2). Chicago Booth's press release framed it as "watching how-to videos on YouTube gives people false sense of expertise." — [Chicago Booth press release](https://www.chicagobooth.edu/media-relations-and-communications/press-releases/easier-seen-than-done-watching-how-to-videos-on-youtube-gives-people-false-sense-of-expertise)
- [PRIMARY] Carpenter et al. (2013): fluent video lecturers boost perceived, not actual, learning (see A2). — [Royal Holloway record](https://pure.royalholloway.ac.uk/en/publications/the-effect-of-instructor-fluency-on-students-perceptions-of-instr/)
- [PRIMARY] Szpunar et al. (2013): building tests into video lectures cut mind-wandering from ~40% to 19% and raised final test scores from 68% to 90% (see A5). — [PMC3631699](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3631699/)
- [PRIMARY] Bastani et al. (2025, *PNAS*; 2024 preprint "Generative AI Can Harm Learning"): a field experiment with ~1,000 high-school math students.
  - With **unrestricted GPT-4 ("GPT Base")**, practice performance rose 48%, but when access was removed, exam performance was **17% lower** than for students who never had access. Students used it as a "crutch."
  - A **"GPT Tutor"** with learning safeguards (hints, not answers) raised practice performance 127% and **largely mitigated** the harm.
  
  — [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=4895486); [RePEc/PNAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/nas/journl/v122y2025pe2422633122.html); [PsyPost](https://www.psypost.org/unrestricted-generative-ai-harms-high-school-math-learning-by-acting-as-a-crutch/)

### Inferences
- For a creator who learns mainly from YouTube, courses and AI: **the ratio of doing to watching is the key variable**. A simple rule: for every tutorial or breakdown watched, produce something (a recreated edit, a rewritten hook, a title variant set) before the next one.
- AI should be set up as a *tutor* (asking you questions, critiquing your attempt, giving hints) rather than an *answer machine* during practice. Otherwise performance during practice improves while independent skill falls (Bastani et al.). This matters directly for the user's goals in scriptwriting and AI prompting.
- "Feeling like I learned a lot from that video" is weak evidence. The test is whether the skill can be performed unaided a day or more later.

### Gaps
- No controlled study was found on learning creative software (e.g., video editors) from tutorials specifically. The extrapolation rests on Kardas & O'Brien's motor and game skills plus the STEM evidence.
- Freeman et al. covers undergraduate STEM classrooms, not self-directed adult learning.

## B1. Deliberate practice: definition, critiques, and what matters besides practice volume

### Takeaway
Deliberate practice works and is necessary, but the amount of practice explains a modest share of skill differences. That share is about 12% overall in the main meta-analysis, varying from ~26% in games to under 1% in professions, and much less among elites. The original "it's mostly practice" claim and the 10,000-hours framing are overstated. Practice *quality* and structure, starting point, cognitive abilities, and the predictability of the domain also matter. Ericsson's camp disputes the critiques mainly on definitions.

### Cited Findings
- [PRIMARY] Ericsson, Krampe & Tesch-Römer (1993, *Psychological Review*) defined deliberate practice as individualized, solitary practice in classical music directed by a qualified teacher: "a very specific activity designed for an individual by a skilled teacher explicitly to improve performance." — [Macnamara & Maitra 2019 (describing the original), PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6731745); [CogSci summary](https://www.jimdavies.org/summaries/ericsson1993.html)
- [POPULAR] Ericsson & Pool, *Peak* (2016), separate "purposeful practice" from "deliberate practice":
  - **Purposeful practice:** specific, proximate goals; full focus; feedback; difficulty just outside the comfort zone.
  - **Deliberate practice:** purposeful practice in a well-developed field with objective performance measures, established training methods and teachers who can design the practice. Both build better *mental representations*.
  
  — [Andy Matuschak's notes on Peak (PRACTITIONER notes)](https://notes.andymatuschak.org/z9uHnUB5sQeGcEmmmqEGqBV); [Peak notes](https://notes.andymatuschak.org/Peak_-_Ericsson_and_Pool)
- [META] Macnamara, Hambrick & Oswald (2014, *Psychological Science*): 88 studies, 11,135 participants. Deliberate practice explained ~**12%** of variance in performance overall:

  | Domain | Variance explained |
  |---|---|
  | Games | 26% |
  | Music | 21% |
  | Sports | 18% |
  | Education | 4% |
  | Professions | <1% |

  Macnamara: "Deliberate practice is unquestionably important, but not nearly as important as proponents of the view have claimed." — [APS press release](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/becoming-an-expert-takes-more-than-practice.html); [Princeton news](https://www.princeton.edu/news/2014/07/03/becoming-expert-takes-more-practice)
- [META] Macnamara, Moreau & Hambrick (2016, *Perspectives on Psychological Science*), sports, 52 datasets: deliberate practice explained **18%** of variance overall but only **1% among elite athletes**. Higher-skill athletes **did not** start their sport earlier. — [Purdue PDF](https://hhs.purdue.edu/skill-learning-and-performance-lab/wp-content/uploads/sites/43/2024/08/macnamara-et-al-2016-the-relationship-between-deliberate-practice-and-performance-in-sports-a-meta-analysis.pdf); [CWRU news](https://case.edu/news/sports-practice-accounts-just-1-percent-performance-differences-among-elite-athletes)
- [PRIMARY] Macnamara & Maitra (2019, *Royal Society Open Science*): a replication of the 1993 violinist study with 39 violinists and double-blind skill ratings. The core finding was **not** confirmed. Deliberate practice accounted for ~26% of variance (vs. a much larger figure in the original) and did **not** distinguish the "best" from the "good" violinists; in fact the "good" group reported more practice. Teacher-designed practice was rated less relevant than solo practice. — [PMC6731745](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6731745); [ScienceBlog](https://scienceblog.com/n-the-ten-thousand-hour-study-was-redone-with-thirty-nine-violinists-under-double-blind-conditions-and-the-group-rated-merely-good-had-logged-more-practice-hours-than-the-group-rated-best/)
- [REVIEW] Hambrick et al. (2014), "Facing facts about deliberate practice": in chess and music, deliberate practice accounted for **about one-third of the reliable variance**. "Most of the variance in performance is explained by factors other than deliberate practice," including **starting age, working-memory capacity and genes**. Practice is necessary but not sufficient. — [PMC4101876](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4101876)
- [REVIEW – rebuttal] Ericsson & Harwell (2019, *Frontiers in Psychology*) argue Macnamara et al. measured "structured practice," not deliberate practice as originally defined. Their reanalysis restricted to studies meeting the original criteria estimates practice explains **29%, and 61% after attenuation correction**. They cite Macnamara's overall figure as 14%, while the APS release says 12%, a discrepancy worth noting. — [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.02396/text)

### Inferences
- What matters besides volume, synthesizing these sources:
  1. **Practice quality**: specific goals, feedback, edge-of-ability work, full concentration.
  2. **Domain structure**: practice explains far more in stable, rule-bound domains like games and music than in professions (see B4 on kind vs. wicked environments).
  3. **Starting knowledge and cognitive abilities** (working memory, etc.).
  4. **Access to good mental models and teachers.**
- For the user's skills, most of which are "profession-like" (business, brand), raw hours will differentiate little. **Better feedback loops and better mental models** (extracted from experts and from data) are the leverage points.
- A "Peak-style" deliberate practice regime is hard to build in creator and business domains because there is no established training curriculum. That gap is exactly what Commoncog's practitioner work addresses (B6, B9).

### Gaps
- There is no meta-analysis of practice effects specific to creative, media or entrepreneurial skills. "Professions <1%" mixes heterogeneous jobs (e.g., refereeing, programming).

## B2. Stages of skill learning, the "OK plateau", arrested development, and breaking plateaus

### Takeaway
Skills move from effortful, rule-based performance (cognitive stage) through refinement (associative) to automatic performance (autonomous). Automaticity is efficient but freezes improvement at "good enough." Experts keep improving by deliberately pulling parts of the skill back into conscious, feedback-driven practice.

### Cited Findings
- [REVIEW/classic] Fitts & Posner (1967), three stages of skill learning:
  - **Cognitive:** goals and action sequences are worked out using explicit knowledge.
  - **Associative:** the rate of improvement slows and consistency grows as attentional demands fall.
  - **Autonomous:** the skill is automatic, consistent and efficient, and can be done alongside other tasks.
  
  — [PMC7899063 figure](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7899063/figure/f2); [Pearson study guide](https://www.pearson.com/channels/psychology/study-guides/stages-of-motor-skill-learning-fitts-and)
- [POPULAR] Joshua Foer, *Moonwalking with Einstein* (2011), coined the **"OK Plateau"**: once a skill is autonomous and "good enough," conscious effort moves elsewhere and improvement stops. Drawing on Ericsson, Foer says experts avoid it by **focusing on technique, staying goal-oriented, and getting constant, immediate feedback**, deliberately staying in the "cognitive stage." — [The Marginalian excerpt](https://www.themarginalian.org/2013/10/17/ok-plateau/); [Make: summary](https://makezine.com/article/education/the-ok-plateau)
- [POPULAR/notes] Ericsson & Pool contrast purposeful practice with "naive practice," which plateaus quickly. — [Matuschak notes on purposeful practice](https://notes.andymatuschak.org/z9uHnUB5sQeGcEmmmqEGqBV)

### Inferences
- Ways to break plateaus, drawn from the sources above:
  - **Find the weak sub-skill** and drill it in isolation. This is Young's direct-drill-direct loop (B9).
  - **Raise the bar artificially**, e.g., cut a script 30% shorter at equal clarity, or edit at a faster pacing target.
  - **Get immediate, specific feedback**: retention-graph drop-offs, peer critique, comparison against an expert version.
  - **Reintroduce variety** (interleaving, varied conditions).
- For a creator, autonomy in editing (using the same transitions and the same structures) is likely a plateau signal. Periodically "de-automating" by copying a distinctly different editor's style forces the work back into the cognitive stage.

### Gaps
- The "OK plateau" is a popular-book label, not a formally tested construct. Plateau-breaking methods for creative or business skills lack controlled studies.

## B3. Feedback: what kind helps, and when it backfires

### Takeaway
Feedback helps on average (d ≈ 0.4–0.5), but **over a third of feedback interventions make performance worse**. Feedback works when it is specific, about the task or process, and says where to go next. It fails when it focuses attention on the self (praise, ego, personal comparison) or carries little information.

### Cited Findings
- [META] Kluger & DeNisi (1996, *Psychological Bulletin* 119): 607 effect sizes, 23,663 observations. Average **d = 0.41**, but **over one-third of feedback interventions decreased performance**. Their Feedback Intervention Theory holds that feedback shifts attention among three levels: task learning, task motivation, and meta-task or self processes. Feedback that draws attention toward the self tends to hurt. — [Kluger abstract (HUJI)](https://pluto.huji.ac.il/%7Emskluger/files/Download/Kluger%20&%20%20DeNisi%20abstract.doc); [PDF](https://mrbartonmaths.com/resourcesnew/8.%20Research/Marking%20and%20Feedback/The%20effects%20of%20feedback%20interventions.pdf)
- [REVIEW] Hattie & Timperley (2007, *Review of Educational Research* 77(1)). Effective feedback answers three questions: **Where am I going? How am I going? Where to next?** (feed-up, feed-back, feed-forward). It operates at four levels: task, process, self-regulation, and self, with **feedback about the self (person) the least effective**. — [Hattie & Timperley PDF](https://www.uky.edu/~gmswan3/575/Hattie_Timperly_2007.pdf); [Sandringham Research School](https://researchschool.org.uk/sandringham/news/effective-feedback-the-research-evidence)
- [META] Wisniewski, Zierer & Hattie (2020, *Frontiers in Psychology*): 435 studies, 994 effect sizes, >61,000 participants. Average **d = 0.48**, with significant heterogeneity, and **the more information the feedback contains, the bigger the effect**. — [PMC6987456](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6987456); [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.03087/full)
- [META] Yang et al. (2021): **corrective feedback** after quizzing is a significant moderator of the classroom testing effect. — [HKBU record](https://scholars.hkbu.edu.hk/en/publications/testing-quizzing-boosts-classroom-learning-a-systematic-and-meta-/)

### Inferences
- For YouTube work: analytics (CTR, average view duration, retention dips) are **task-level, information-rich feedback** and should be used heavily. Comments and praise or criticism about *the creator* are self-level and weak or harmful as learning signals.
- Good feedback requests ask "where exactly did you lose interest, and what would you cut?" rather than "is this good?"
- Feedback timing: the evidence here does not settle immediate vs. delayed feedback. For skill drills, faster feedback is the practical default, consistent with Kahneman & Klein's "rapid, unequivocal feedback" condition in B4.

### Gaps
- I did not retrieve a meta-analysis on feedback *timing* (immediate vs. delayed), e.g., Shute 2008 or the Kulik & Kulik work, so timing claims are not evidence-graded here.

## B4. Kind vs. wicked learning environments, and what that means for the user's skills

### Takeaway
Intuitive expertise develops reliably only where the environment is regular (high validity) and gives enough practice with rapid, clear feedback. In "wicked" environments, where feedback is delayed, noisy or misleading, experience alone breeds confidence more than skill. Several of the user's target skills are semi-wicked, and business and brand building are wicked.

### Cited Findings
- [REVIEW] Kahneman & Klein (2009, *American Psychologist*), "Conditions for intuitive expertise: A failure to disagree": judging whether to trust an intuition requires assessing (1) **how predictable (valid) the environment is** and (2) **whether the person had an adequate opportunity to learn its regularities**. A high-validity environment is a **necessary** condition for skilled intuition. — [Kahneman & Klein PDF (Charles U.)](https://dl1.cuni.cz/mod/resource/view.php?id=153128); [McKinsey interview](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/strategy-and-corporate-finance/our-insights/strategic-decisions-when-can-you-trust-your-gut)
- [REVIEW] Hogarth, Lejarraga & Soyer (2015, *Current Directions in Psychological Science* 24(5)), building on Hogarth's *Educating Intuition* (2001). **Kind** environments have a close match between the information you learn from and the situation you must decide in. **Wicked** environments have a mismatch, with feedback that is incomplete, delayed, inaccurate or biased. — [UIB record](https://portalinvestigacio.uib.es/documentos/67fbe1662308cf3c390f5aa7?lang=es)
- [POPULAR] David Epstein, *Range* (2019), popularized kind vs. wicked: in kind environments "rules are clear, patterns repeat, feedback is quick and accurate," while in wicked ones "the rules of the game are often unclear or incomplete… and feedback is often delayed, inaccurate, or both." — [Epstein Substack](https://davidepstein.substack.com/p/how-to-get-better-at-learning-from-experience); [UCL IOE](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/ioe/news/2021/nov/five-things-make-classroom-wicked)
- [META] Macnamara et al. (2014): practice explains most in rule-bound domains (games 26%) and least in professions (<1%). That pattern fits the kind/wicked distinction, though it is my interpretation, not the authors' claim. — [APS press release](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/becoming-an-expert-takes-more-than-practice.html)

### Inferences
My classification of the user's skills (not from any source; based on the criteria above):

| Skill | Kind ↔ Wicked | Why | Implication |
|---|---|---|---|
| Video editing (technical: cuts, audio, color, software fluency) | **Kind** | Immediate visual/audio feedback; clear right and wrong; high repetition | Classic drills and deliberate practice; recreate expert edits shot for shot; spaced practice of shortcuts and techniques |
| Creative editing (pacing, storytelling through the edit) | **Semi-kind** | Retention graphs give data, but it is delayed and confounded by topic and packaging | Compare against expert edits; A/B patterns across many videos; peer review |
| AI prompting | **Mostly kind** | Instant output, cheap iteration; but judging quality needs a good mental model, and models change over time | Rapid iterate-and-compare loops; keep a prompt library with notes on outcomes; re-test when models update |
| Scriptwriting / hooks | **Semi-kind** | Audience retention gives second-by-second signals, but they arrive days later and are confounded | Many small reps (hooks, intros) with feedback; copywork of top scripts; retention-dip analysis |
| YouTube ideation / packaging (titles, thumbnails) | **Semi-wicked** | CTR and views are quantified but noisy (algorithm, timing, small samples), with few reps per week | Record predictions before publishing and compare with outcomes; study many outlier examples (vicarious reps); test thumbnails where possible |
| Business / entrepreneurship | **Wicked** | Sparse, delayed, multi-causal feedback; few repetitions; non-stationary | Case-based learning; extract tacit knowledge from experts (B6); decision journals; pre-mortems; do not trust gut feel built on few cases |
| Brand building | **Wicked** | Very delayed and diffuse feedback | Long-horizon metrics; learn from others' documented case histories; treat intuitions as hypotheses |

- In wicked domains the user should **manufacture kindness**: break the work into sub-skills with faster feedback (e.g., test a single offer, a single hook, or a single cold DM script), and borrow other people's experience at volume through case studies and expert interviews.

### Gaps
- No empirical study classifies content-creation or YouTube work as kind or wicked. The mapping above is reasoned inference.

## B5. Mental representations and chunking

### Takeaway
Experts do not have bigger working memories. They have large libraries of domain-specific patterns ("chunks") that let them perceive, remember and decide in larger units. Building these representations, not accumulating hours, is what separates experts.

### Cited Findings
- [PRIMARY] Chase & Simon (1973, *Cognitive Psychology*), "Perception in chess," building on de Groot: a master reconstructed real game positions after 5 seconds much better than weaker players, but **not random positions**. The interpretation is that experts have an ordinary working-memory span but perceive positions in larger **chunks**. Simon and colleagues' order-of-magnitude estimate is **~50,000 chunks** for masters, comparable to an educated adult's vocabulary. — [Andy Matuschak notes](https://notes.andymatuschak.org/Chase_and_Simon_-_Perception_in_chess); [Chess Programming wiki](https://chessprogramming.org/William_Chase); [Simon 1973 PDF](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/chess/1973-simon.pdf)
- [REVIEW] Kalyuga et al. (2003): schemas in long-term memory are what let experts get around working-memory limits, which is also why novice-oriented instruction becomes redundant for experts. — [UOW record](https://ro.uow.edu.au/edupapers/136)
- [POPULAR] Ericsson & Pool, *Peak*: purposeful and deliberate practice aims to build better **mental representations**, i.e., organized patterns for perceiving and planning in the domain. — [Matuschak notes on Peak](https://notes.andymatuschak.org/Peak_-_Ericsson_and_Pool)

### Inferences
- For a creator, "chunks" are things like hook archetypes, story structures, title formulas, thumbnail compositions, transition patterns and offer structures. Collecting and *labelling* many examples (a swipe file with annotations of *why* it works), then practicing retrieval and generation from those labels, should build these representations faster than watching passively.
- Pattern libraries only form when the patterns are real. Chase & Simon's random-board result implies chunk-building pays off in domains with recurring structure (kind or semi-kind). In wicked domains, the "patterns" may be noise.

### Gaps
- No direct studies of chunking in video editing or copywriting were retrieved.

## B6. Tacit knowledge and learning from experts: cognitive task analysis, apprenticeship, imitation and copywork

### Takeaway
Much expert knowledge is tacit: experts leave out most of their decision-making when asked to explain it. Methods that **extract** or **imitate** expert performance include cognitive task analysis, cognitive apprenticeship (modeling, coaching, scaffolding, fading), and structured copywork like Franklin's method. These are especially valuable in creative and business domains where no formal deliberate-practice curriculum exists.

### Cited Findings
- [REVIEW/conference] Clark, Feldon, Yates and colleagues on Cognitive Task Analysis (CTA): when experts describe how they perform complex tasks, they **omit about 70% of the decisions** they make, because much of their knowledge is automated procedural knowledge. CTA is a structured interview and observation method for capturing it, and instruction based on it is more complete. — [Clark & Feldon chapter record (USU)](https://digitalcommons.usu.edu/itls_facpub/454); [LearnLab wiki](https://learnlab.org/research/wiki/index.php/Cognitive_task_analysis)
- [REVIEW/chapter] Collins, Brown & Newman (1989), "Cognitive Apprenticeship: Teaching the Crafts of Reading, Writing, and Mathematics." Makes experts' hidden cognitive strategies visible through **modeling, coaching, scaffolding and fading**, plus articulation, reflection and exploration. It works best when skills are taught in authentic context. — [Wikipedia overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cognitive_apprenticeship); [AFT article](https://www.aft.org/node/18441); [Collins webinar PDF](https://www.isls-naples.psy.uni-muenchen.de/intro/all-webinars/collins/cognitive-apprenticeship.pdf)
- [PRIMARY historical source] Benjamin Franklin's *Autobiography* describes his writing method:
  - He took *Spectator* essays, wrote short hints of each sentence's meaning, set them aside for days, then **rewrote the essay from the hints without looking** and compared his version with the original to correct his faults.
  - He turned the prose into verse and later back into prose, to build vocabulary.
  - He **jumbled his hints** and later re-ordered them, to learn structure.
  
  — [ushistory.org Autobiography p.6](https://www.ushistory.org/FRANKLIN/autobiography/page06.htm); [Rasmusen excerpt](https://rasmusen.org/w/04.02.10a.htm)
- [PRACTITIONER] Cedric Chin's Commoncog argues that for business and career practitioners, research on *extracting tacit expertise* (Naturalistic Decision Making: CTA, the Critical Decision Method, the Recognition-Primed Decision model, *Accelerated Expertise*, Cognitive Flexibility Theory for ill-structured domains) is more useful than "pop" deliberate practice. His reason is that DP needs someone to have already designed the training exercises, which business lacks. — [Commoncog Expertise hub](https://www.commoncog.com/expertise); [Tacit expertise extraction article](https://commoncog.com/tacit-expertise-extraction-software-engineer/)
- [PRIMARY] Kardas & O'Brien (2018): watching experts alone builds overconfidence, not skill. Imitation has to involve **doing**. — [Chicago Booth](https://www.chicagobooth.edu/media-relations-and-communications/press-releases/easier-seen-than-done-watching-how-to-videos-on-youtube-gives-people-false-sense-of-expertise)

### Inferences
- **Franklin's method maps almost directly onto modern retrieval and generation research**: summarize, delay (spacing), reconstruct from memory (retrieval/generation), compare to the expert (feedback). Applications for the user:
  - **Scripts:** outline a top creator's script into beat-level hints, wait 2–3 days, rewrite it from the hints, compare line by line.
  - **Editing:** watch a 30-second expert sequence, list the cuts and effects as hints, rebuild it from scratch, then overlay and compare.
  - **Titles/thumbnails:** write your own title for an outlier video's topic before revealing theirs, then compare.
- When talking to experts (clients, mentors, other creators), ask CTA-style questions ("walk me through the last time you… what did you notice? what would a novice miss? what alternatives did you reject?") rather than "what's your advice?", which yields the ~30% they can easily put into words.
- Cognitive apprenticeship suggests looking for a coach or mentor relationship with **fading** support, rather than consuming courses indefinitely.

### Gaps
- The ~70% omission figure comes from conference and chapter sources (Clark, Feldon, Yates), and the primary study was not retrieved. No controlled trial of Franklin-style copywork was found; it is a historical anecdote that aligns with retrieval and generation evidence.

## B7. Transfer of learning: near vs. far, and why "learning about" doesn't become "doing"

### Takeaway
Training transfers well to closely similar tasks (near transfer) and very poorly to dissimilar ones (far transfer). Practicing generic "brain skills" or consuming knowledge *about* a skill does not reliably produce the ability to *do* it. Practice has to closely resemble the target performance.

### Cited Findings
- [META] Sala & Gobet (2017, *Developmental Psychology* 53(4)): working-memory training in children produced near-transfer **g = 0.46** but far-transfer only **g = 0.12**. Effects shrank as study quality rose (random allocation, active controls): "far-transfer rarely occurs and its effects are minimal." — [Liverpool repository](https://livrepository.liverpool.ac.uk/3006842/); [PDF](https://Www.Gwern.net/doc/dual-n-back/2017-sala.pdf)
- [META] Sala & Gobet (2019), second-order meta-analysis, "Near and far transfer in cognitive training": similarly negative far-transfer evidence across chess, music, video-game, working-memory and exergame training. — [Fujita Health record](https://pure.fujita-hu.ac.jp/en/publications/near-and-far-transfer-in-cognitive-training-a-second-order-meta-a/)
- [PRIMARY] Kardas & O'Brien (2018): knowledge gained by watching did not transfer to performance. — [PDF](https://gwern.net/doc/psychology/cognitive-bias/illusion-of-depth/2018-kardas.pdf)
- [META] Yang et al. (2021): quiz benefits were larger when quiz format and material **matched** the final test (format consistency and material matching moderators), a transfer-appropriate pattern. — [HKBU](https://scholars.hkbu.edu.hk/en/publications/testing-quizzing-boosts-classroom-learning-a-systematic-and-meta-/)
- [POPULAR] Scott Young's "directness" principle: learn by doing the thing in the context where you will use it. — [Shortform summary](https://www.shortform.com/blog/ultralearning-principles/)

### Inferences
- "Learning about business" from podcasts, or learning "about" editing from breakdowns, will mostly not transfer into doing. The practice task should look like the real task: real scripts, real edits, real sales conversations, real launches at small scale.
- General "focus training" apps are unlikely to transfer to focus during actual learning. Shaping the real learning environment is the better-evidenced route (A5).

### Gaps
- I did not retrieve Barnett & Ceci's (2002) transfer taxonomy, or evidence on teaching for transfer (e.g., varied practice, analogical comparison). These are known techniques but are unsourced here.

## B8. Motivation and consistency: habits, self-determination, goals, small wins (brief)

### Takeaway
Habits take weeks to months to become automatic (median ~66 days, very wide range), with the biggest gains early on. Motivation lasts longer when the learner has autonomy, a sense of growing competence and connection to others. Specific, challenging goals beat "do your best," and visible progress on meaningful work is the strongest day-to-day motivator.

### Cited Findings
- [PRIMARY] Lally, van Jaarsveld, Potts & Wardle (2010, *European Journal of Social Psychology*): 96 participants each repeated one new daily health behavior for 84 days. Automaticity followed an **asymptotic curve**, with early repetitions adding the most. The median time to the plateau was **66 days, range 18–254 days**, partly extrapolated. Caveat: only a subset (~39) had data that fit the model well. — [BPS Research Digest](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/how-form-habit); [Behavioral Scientist critique](https://www.thebehavioralscientist.com/articles/how-long-to-form-a-habit)
- [REVIEW] Ryan & Deci (2000, *American Psychologist* 55(1)), Self-Determination Theory: three basic needs, **autonomy, competence and relatedness**, underlie intrinsic motivation and well-being. — [Wikipedia overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-determination_theory)
- [REVIEW] Locke & Latham (2002, *American Psychologist*), 35 years of goal-setting research: **specific, difficult goals** produce higher performance than easy, vague ("do your best") or no goals. — [Locke & Latham 2002 PDF](https://goal-lab.psych.umn.edu/orgpsych/2020/readings/5.%20Motivation/Locke%20&%20Latham%20(2002).pdf)
- [POPULAR, research-based] Amabile & Kramer, *The Progress Principle* (2011): ~12,000 diary entries from 238 employees in 7 companies. "Of all the events that characterized the best inner work life days, by far the most prominent was making progress," and setbacks most characterized the worst days. — [HBS Working Knowledge](https://hbswk.hbs.edu/item/6685.html); [HBR Store](https://store.hbr.org/product/the-progress-principle-using-small-wins-to-ignite-joy-engagement-and-creativity-at-work/10106)

### Inferences
- Plan for a 2–3-month "habit ramp" for a daily learning block, not 21 days. Expect it to vary a lot from person to person.
- Make progress visible: a daily log of shipped reps (hooks written, edits recreated, prompts tested), tied to specific stretch goals ("write 5 hooks a day and get 1 rated ≥8/10 by a peer").
- Autonomy and competence: choose projects the user actually cares about, sized so wins come early. That links to the desirable-difficulty caveat that difficulty must be manageable.

### Gaps
- Lally's frequently cited claim that a single missed day did not materially affect habit formation was not verified in the sources fetched. Goal-setting effect sizes were not retrieved.

## B9. Practical frameworks for self-learners (popular and practitioner)

### Takeaway
Popular frameworks largely repackage the evidence above: directness, drilling weak points, retrieval, feedback, spacing, learning from experts. They are useful as operating systems, but they are not themselves tested. Commoncog's tacit-knowledge approach is the most relevant for business and creator skills.

### Cited Findings
- [POPULAR] Scott Young, *Ultralearning* (2019), nine principles: **metalearning** (map how to learn the skill), **focus**, **directness** (learn by doing in the real context), **drill** (isolate weak points), **retrieval**, **feedback**, **retention** (spacing, overlearning), **intuition/deeper knowledge**, **experimentation**. Core loop: "direct–drill–direct": practice the whole skill, isolate weak links and drill them, then reintegrate. — [Shortform summary](https://www.shortform.com/blog/ultralearning-principles/); [book summary PDF](https://public.summaries.com/files/8-page-summary/ultralearning.pdf)
- [PRACTITIONER] Commoncog (Cedric Chin) groups expertise acceleration into three traditions:
  1. **Deliberate practice** (hard to apply when nobody has designed the training).
  2. **Naturalistic Decision Making** (extract tacit expertise from real experts via CTA, the Critical Decision Method and the Recognition-Primed Decision model; *Accelerated Expertise*; Cognitive Flexibility Theory for ill-structured domains such as business).
  3. **Trial-and-error learning**, including how experts discard old mental models (Cognitive Transformation Theory).
  
  — [Commoncog Expertise](https://www.commoncog.com/expertise)
- [POPULAR] Ericsson & Pool, *Peak*: purposeful practice (specific goals, focus, feedback, beyond the comfort zone). — [Matuschak notes](https://notes.andymatuschak.org/z9uHnUB5sQeGcEmmmqEGqBV)
- [POPULAR] Epstein, *Range*: breadth and sampling pay off in wicked domains. — [Epstein Substack](https://davidepstein.substack.com/p/how-to-get-better-at-learning-from-experience)
- [POPULAR] Foer, *Moonwalking with Einstein*: the "OK plateau." — [The Marginalian](https://www.themarginalian.org/2013/10/17/ok-plateau/)

### Inferences
An integrated protocol for this user (synthesis; each element traces to evidence above):
1. **Metalearning first (1–2 hrs per skill):** list the sub-skills, then find 5–10 "masters" and their best work (expertise reversal: novices learn from worked examples).
2. **Project-based core ("direct")**: ship real outputs weekly (a video, a script, a set of thumbnails) so practice matches the target performance (transfer).
3. **Drills on the weakest sub-skill**, using Franklin-style copywork: summarize the expert work, delay, reconstruct, compare (retrieval + generation + feedback).
4. **Retrieval-punctuated input**: no tutorial longer than ~5–10 minutes without a pause to reproduce it; AI in "tutor mode," not answer mode (Szpunar; Bastani).
5. **Spaced, interleaved review** of the swipe-file patterns: hooks, title formulas, editing techniques (spacing; interleaving for discrimination).
6. **Feedback loops matched to kind vs. wicked**: analytics and peer critique for the semi-kind skills; prediction logs, case studies and expert interviews (CTA questions) for business and brand.
7. **Focus hygiene**: phone out of the room, finish one unit before switching, limit short-form scrolling during learning blocks (A5).
8. **Sustainability**: specific stretch goals, a visible progress log, sleep protected, a 66-day ramp (B8).

### Gaps
- I found no peer-reviewed evaluation of *Ultralearning* or Commoncog methods as packages. Project-based learning evidence (e.g., meta-analyses on PBL) was not retrieved in this session, so "project-based core" rests on transfer and directness reasoning rather than PBL-specific effect sizes.
- Newport's *Deep Work* claims were not sourced here.

## C. Integrated implications: how attention/focus links to learning for a learner prone to daydreaming

### Takeaway
For this user, the techniques with the strongest evidence (retrieval, spacing, active production) **double as attention tools**. Frequent output demands cut mind-wandering roughly in half in video learning, and active formats beat passive watching even though they feel worse. The weakest-evidence habits (rewatching, rereading, ambient tutorials, multitasking) are also the ones most compatible with drifting into daydreams.

### Cited Findings
- [PRIMARY] Building tests into video lectures: mind-wandering 19% vs. ~40%; final test 90% vs. 68%. — [Szpunar et al. 2013, PMC3631699](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3631699/)
- [META] Momentary mind-wandering correlates r ≈ −0.21 with comprehension, while trait questionnaires show almost no correlation. The *state* during learning is what matters. — [Bonifacci et al., PMC9971160](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9971160/)
- [META] Heavier short-form video use correlates with poorer attention (r = −0.38) and inhibitory control (r = −0.41). — [PsyPost on Nguyen et al. 2025](https://www.psypost.org/large-meta-analysis-links-tiktok-and-instagram-reels-to-poorer-cognitive-and-mental-health/)
- [PRIMARY] Unfinished tasks leave attention residue; finishing under time pressure reduces it. — [Leroy 2009 via strategy+business](https://strategy-business.com/article/re00088)
- [PRIMARY] Active learning yields more learning but feels like less. — [Deslauriers et al. 2019](https://openlab.citytech.cuny.edu/gamelab/files/2023/01/Deslauriersa-et-al-2019.pdf)
- [PRIMARY] Unrestricted AI help raises practice performance but lowers independent performance afterwards; tutor-style AI does not. — [Bastani et al., SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=4895486)

### Inferences
- **Design learning so there is no "passive slot" for daydreams to fill.** Use short input segments, each followed by an output demand: write, rebuild, explain aloud, quiz.
- Use **time-boxed sprints with a defined deliverable**, e.g., a 25–45-minute block ending in a finished hook set. Finishing creates closure (Leroy), and the deadline adds productive time pressure.
- Treat the discomfort of active practice as a sign it is working, not a sign of failure (Deslauriers; Bjork). This reframe matters for someone whose daydreaming may be a way of escaping effortful states. That last point is inference; MD mechanisms are covered elsewhere in this project.
- **Separate consumption and creation**: schedule deliberate "research" viewing of outlier videos with a notes template, rather than ambient scrolling.

### Gaps
- No study was found testing these learning techniques specifically in people with maladaptive daydreaming or ADHD-like attention profiles. All MD-specific recommendations are extrapolations from general mind-wandering research.
